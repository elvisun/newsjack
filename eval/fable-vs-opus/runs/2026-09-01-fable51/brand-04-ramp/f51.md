**Companies are paying $342,000 a year for software nobody uses, Ramp's beta finds**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A data or finance-ops reporter at a CFO trade outlet, procurement newsletter, or B2B SaaS business publication that runs "how much are companies wasting" pieces. The number is the story, but only with context: what share was unused seats versus auto-renewals, what size companies were in the beta, and whether the savings were realized or merely identified. Not for consumer tech press, general AI desks, or product-review outlets.
- **Why now:** The agent is launching now, and the 200-company beta is the first public dataset behind it.
- **Decay:** week. The launch itself is a 24-hour event, but a dataset about SaaS waste holds up as a context piece for several days if the cut is clean.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, scale plus variation. The protagonist is the number, and the reader is a finance leader benchmarking their own waste. The other angles are about who loses, what the savings reveal about AI, and how the agent is governed.
- **Proof it needs:** the median, not just the mean; the split between unused seats, duplicates, and auto-renewals; company size bands in the beta; how much was actually cancelled or renegotiated after approval versus flagged; whether any beta customer will be named.
- **Facts it rests on:** 200-company beta; average of $342,000 in annual savings per company; savings mostly from unused seats and auto-renewals; the agent scans spend and flags duplicate and unused seats.

**The auto-renewal has an adversary now: Ramp's agent drafts the cancellation email**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A reporter covering the SaaS business model, net revenue retention, and vendor pricing at an enterprise-software business outlet or a SaaS-industry newsletter. The protagonist is the software vendor, not Ramp: an agent that hunts unused seats and auto-renewals across a large spend base attacks revenue lines vendors have quietly relied on. That beat will want to know how vendors respond when the cancellation email is machine-drafted. Not for fintech product reporters, personal finance, or local business press.
- **Why now:** The launch puts a specific, scaled tool behind a threat SaaS vendors could previously treat as hypothetical.
- **Decay:** week. The vendor-side reaction story can run after launch day, especially if a vendor comments on record.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, conflict. The reader cares because someone loses money. The data angle tells finance teams what they waste; this one tells vendors who is coming for it.
- **Proof it needs:** how many cancellation or renegotiation emails the beta actually sent and how vendors responded; at least one example of a renegotiated contract, anonymized if necessary; whether drafted emails disclose that an AI wrote them; a Ramp position on vendor pushback.
- **Facts it rests on:** the agent drafts cancellation or renegotiation emails the finance team approves; savings came mostly from unused seats and auto-renewals; Ramp processes over $80B in annualized purchase volume.

**The AI agent's biggest savings didn't come from negotiating. They came from cancelling.**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** An enterprise-AI reporter or columnist at a business-tech outlet who is skeptical of agent hype and wants evidence of where agents actually deliver value. The prevailing pitch for agentic AI is sophisticated work like negotiation. Ramp's own beta says most of the money was in housekeeping: seats nobody used and renewals nobody noticed. That is a usable, evidence-backed corrective. Not for CFO trade outlets that want the savings number itself, and not for vendor-side SaaS reporters.
- **Why now:** The launch supplies the evidence; without the beta data this is just an opinion.
- **Decay:** week. It rides the launch but works as a reported column for days afterward.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens. The protagonist is the agentic-AI narrative, and the fact cuts against it. The data angle reports the number; this angle argues what the number means about AI.
- **Proof it needs:** the dollar split between cancellation-driven and renegotiation-driven savings; how many renegotiations succeeded and what discount they produced; a Ramp spokesperson willing to say on record that hygiene beat haggling.
- **Facts it rests on:** savings were mostly from unused seats and auto-renewals; the agent both flags waste and drafts renegotiation emails; average of $342,000 per company across 200 companies.

**Ramp puts an AI agent to work on vendor contracts, but a human still hits send**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A reporter covering finance controls, AI governance, or agent deployment in regulated back-office functions at a CFO trade outlet or an enterprise-AI publication. The interesting design choice is the approval gate: the agent drafts outbound emails to third parties, and finance approves each one. This beat is tracking how companies let agents act externally without losing control, and the coming enterprise tier with custom approval workflows makes the governance layer the product. Not for the vendor-side SaaS beat, and not for general AI-launch roundups.
- **Why now:** The launch, plus the stated plan for an enterprise tier with custom approval workflows later this year.
- **Decay:** week. Governance-of-agents pieces are trend stories, not day-of news.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the finance team as operator of an agent. The story is how the agent is controlled, not how much it saves or whom it threatens.
- **Proof it needs:** what the finance team sees before approving; whether the agent can send anything without approval; how approvals are logged; what "custom approval workflows" will include in the enterprise tier; any beta customer describing how they set the gate.
- **Facts it rests on:** the agent drafts cancellation or renegotiation emails the finance team approves; an enterprise tier with custom approval workflows is coming later this year.

**Ramp gives away software-spend auditing for free, and plans to charge enterprises later**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A fintech or B2B-software business reporter covering spend-management platforms, bundling, and pricing strategy. The story is Ramp absorbing a standalone job, SaaS spend auditing, as a no-cost feature of a spend platform, with paid enterprise tiering to follow. This reporter can test whether that squeezes anyone selling this capability on its own and why a platform built on spend would help customers spend less. Not for AI desks, CFO trade outlets focused on the savings number, or vendor-side SaaS reporters.
- **Why now:** The launch, and the explicit free-now, enterprise-tier-later structure.
- **Decay:** week. Pricing and bundling analysis holds for a news cycle after launch.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up to the category. The protagonist is the spend-management market and Ramp's position in it, not the number, the vendors, or the agent.
- **Proof it needs:** Ramp's on-record rationale for bundling at no extra cost; an answer to how Ramp earns when customers cut spend; a user-supplied comparison of what standalone SaaS-management tools charge; enterprise-tier pricing or at least its scope.
- **Facts it rests on:** the feature is included for existing customers at no extra cost; an enterprise tier with custom approval workflows is coming later this year; Ramp processes over $80B in annualized purchase volume.

**Refused angles**

- "Ramp's revolutionary AI agent transforms SaaS spend management" — `slop`.
- "Ramp's agent will save companies $68 million" — `hallucinated_fact`. Multiplying the average by 200 companies produces savings the beta identified, not savings anyone has confirmed were realized.
- "How one CFO cut $342,000 with an AI agent" — `hallucinated_fact`. No customer is named; writing this would require inventing one.
- "The Ramp founders' bet on agentic finance" — `no_journalist_shape`. No founder, executive, or backstory facts were supplied, so there is no profile to write.
- "Ramp's enterprise tier signals a new phase for AI in finance" — `no_why_now_but_required`. "Later this year" is a promise, not an event.
- "Ramp joins the AI agent race" — `no_journalist_shape`. Every launch this year says this; it reads as a topic, not a story.
- "Finance teams get an AI cancel button for SaaS" — `duplicate`. Same conflict story as the auto-renewal angle, weaker protagonist.
- "Ramp's agent and the coming AI-versus-AI procurement war" — `hallucinated_fact`. Nothing in the update says vendors are deploying agents to respond.
- Any news-peg angle — none attempted. No current signal was supplied, and forcing one is the failure mode this skill refuses.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Is $342,000 a mean pulled up by a few large companies? Reporters will ask for the median and the company-size mix, and the data angle collapses without them.
- How much of that figure was actually cancelled or renegotiated after finance approval, versus flagged and left alone? "Found" and "saved" are different headlines.
- Will any of the 200 beta customers go on record? Without one, three of the five angles rest entirely on Ramp's own numbers.
- Did any vendor accept a renegotiated price, and by how much? If renegotiation barely worked, say so before a reporter discovers it.
- Do the drafted emails tell the vendor an AI wrote them? Expect that question from the vendor-side beat.
- Is the launch live at 16:00 UTC today or still under embargo? The 24-hour launch clock starts when it is public, and pitching before then is an embargo problem.
- What is Ramp's answer when a reporter asks why a platform that processes $80B in spend wants customers to spend less?

**Next step:** `journalist-fit-check`. Five viable shapes exist; pick one, then resolve real names against it. If it is the data angle, get the median and the realized-versus-identified split first, or the pitch will not survive the first reply.
