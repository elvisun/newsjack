**On will rent you a running shoe you can never own**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail or consumer-business reporter at a trade outlet covering apparel and footwear business models — the person who writes about rental, resale, and subscription economics in fashion. The hook is ownership, not sustainability: a profitable public company is telling customers they will never own the product they run in, and mailing it back is a condition of the deal. That reporter can test whether subscription-only footwear is a real category or a marketing wrapper. Not for general startup press, running-gear review desks, or climate policy reporters.
- **Why now:** The pilot has ended and the subscription is going nationwide today. The scale change is the news; the pilot version already had its moment.
- **Decay:** 24hr — a nationwide product launch from a public company runs on the standard launch cycle unless a reporter picks up the category thread.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — the story is the ownership model across footwear, not On's shoe or On's recycling claim. The other angles drop to the material, to the disclosure gap, and to the runner.
- **Proof it needs:** the monthly price and contract terms; what happens if a subscriber doesn't return the shoe; how many pilot subscribers converted; nationwide availability footprint (all 50 states? online only?); whether any competitor operates a comparable footwear subscription.
- **Facts it rests on:** subscribers pay a monthly fee, run in the shoe, then mail it back rather than owning it; the subscription was previously a limited pilot; it is now launching nationwide; On is publicly traded and profitable.

**A profitable public company is scaling a circular shoe without saying what share actually gets recycled**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A sustainability-claims or greenwashing-accountability reporter at a business or climate-business outlet — the beat that audits corporate circularity claims against disclosed numbers. The prevailing belief is that closed-loop take-back programs are the proof point of a genuine circular business; the counter-evidence is sitting in this announcement, where a company confident enough to go nationwide has not disclosed a recycling rate or per-unit economics. That's a documented gap in a company's own launch, not a hostile assumption. Not for product-review desks, running press, or lifestyle features.
- **Why now:** The nationwide scale-up is the peg. A pilot with undisclosed numbers is a footnote; a national program with undisclosed numbers is a story.
- **Decay:** week — the accountability angle survives past launch day and gets stronger if On declines to answer.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — every other angle treats the loop as the product; this one treats the missing denominator as the story. Requires On to be willing to lose the framing.
- **Proof it needs:** On's own recycling rate, or an on-record statement that it won't disclose one; what percentage of pilot shoes actually came back; where the grinding and remanufacturing happens and by whom; how much recovered material goes into a new Cyclon versus downcycled uses; independent verification or third-party audit if one exists. Without at least the return rate, this angle is a question, not a story — and pitching it means volunteering your weakest number.
- **Facts it rests on:** the shoe is mailed back to be ground down and remade; the company says it wants to close the loop on shoe waste; recycling-rate and per-unit economics are not disclosed; On is publicly traded and profitable.

**The castor bean is quietly becoming a running-shoe raw material**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A materials or supply-chain reporter at a textiles, chemicals, or manufacturing trade outlet covering bio-based feedstocks replacing petrochemical inputs. The protagonist here is the input, not the brand: castor-bean-derived material going into a nationally distributed shoe from a fast-growing athletic company is a demand signal for an agricultural supply chain. Not for consumer running press, sustainability marketing outlets, or general tech reporters.
- **Why now:** Nationwide distribution changes the volume question — a pilot doesn't move a feedstock market, a national program might. That's the honest hook, and it's softer than the launch itself.
- **Decay:** week — feedstock and supply-chain pieces run on a slower clock than launch news.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the supply chain as protagonist — a completely different cast from the ownership angle and the disclosure angle. Nobody in this story is a runner or a shopper.
- **Proof it needs:** what percentage of the shoe is castor-based versus conventional material; where the castor is sourced and whether On can name a supplier; unit volumes or a production forecast; whether the ground-down material can go back into a new Cyclon or only into lower-grade uses; any independent lifecycle assessment.
- **Facts it rests on:** the Cyclon is made from a castor-bean-based material; the shoe is ground down and remade; the subscription is going nationwide; On is one of the fastest-growing athletic brands.

**What runners actually give up when the shoe belongs to the brand**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A running-gear or endurance-sports reporter at a running publication or an outdoor-gear desk, writing for people who log real mileage and have opinions about when a shoe is dead. The tension is concrete: runners track shoe mileage obsessively, and this model hands the retirement decision to the brand. That reporter can test the shoe and the terms together, which is a review plus a business question. Not for business desks, climate reporters, or materials trade press.
- **Why now:** No urgent hook beyond national availability making the shoe reachable for a reviewer's readership. This is the least time-pressured angle in the set — honest about that.
- **Decay:** month — gear-and-model pieces run on a review calendar, not a news cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the runner as protagonist, at the bottom of the ladder — one person, one pair, one mileage log. Structurally opposite to the category angle above it.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named pilot subscriber willing to talk about the swap experience; the return trigger — mileage, months, or subscriber choice; how long a shipment cycle takes and whether there's a gap without shoes; pilot retention or churn; review pairs for the reporter.
- **Facts it rests on:** subscribers pay a monthly fee, run in the shoe, then mail it back to be ground down and remade rather than owning it; the subscription was previously a limited pilot and is now nationwide.

**Refused angles**

- "On revolutionizes sustainable footwear with world-first circular subscription" — `slop`. Undefended superlative, press-release shape, and "world-first" is a claim nothing in the facts supports.
- "On's Cyclon proves consumers are ready to stop owning things" — `hallucinated_fact`. There is no pilot conversion data, no subscriber count, no demand evidence in the facts. The pilot ending and a nationwide launch is not proof of consumer appetite.
- "How On is winning the sustainability race against Nike and Adidas" — `hallucinated_fact`. No competitor is named in the facts, no comparative data exists, and naming rivals to manufacture conflict is invention.
- "On's growth story: how a profitable public company keeps scaling" — `off-beat`. That is an equities story with no news in it; the subscription is a product decision, not a disclosed financial event. Markets desks want numbers this update doesn't have.
- "The circular economy comes to running shoes" — `duplicate` of the category-creation angle, with a weaker protagonist and no ownership tension. Same lens, same altitude, softer headline.
- "Why sustainability leaders should watch On's Cyclon subscription" — `no_journalist_shape`. "Sustainability leaders" is an audience, not a beat. No reporter covers that.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- What is the actual return rate from the pilot? If a meaningful share of pilot shoes never came back, the entire closed-loop framing is doing work the data won't support, and a good reporter will ask this in the first email. Know the number before you pitch — including whether you're willing to say it.
- What percentage of a returned Cyclon becomes a new Cyclon, versus being downcycled into something else? "Ground down and remade" covers a wide range of outcomes, some of which aren't a closed loop at all.
- Is there a named pilot subscriber who'll go on record? Without one, the runner angle collapses and the category angle loses its only human texture.
- You're withholding per-unit economics. That's your call, but the ownership-model angle is fundamentally an economics story — a business reporter will ask whether the subscription is profitable or subsidized, and "we don't disclose" invites the assumption that it's subsidized.
- Does "nationwide" mean all 50 states, direct-to-consumer only, or through retail partners? Reporters will check, and a mismatch between the word and the footprint is an avoidable own goal.
- Do you have any independent verification of the recycling process — a third-party audit, an LCA, a named remanufacturing partner? Sustainability-accountability reporters will treat an unverified circularity claim as the story rather than the background.

**Next step:** `journalist-fit-check` — four viable shapes exist across four genuinely different desks. Pick one angle first (the ownership/category angle is the strongest and needs the least missing proof), then resolve real names against it. Before pitching the contrarian angle, get the return rate; without it you're pitching a question you can't answer, which is how a launch story becomes an accountability story about you.
