**Notion adds customer-held encryption keys as four of the five largest US banks run pilots**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** An enterprise-software reporter at a business or B2B tech outlet who covers the collaboration-suite fight — Notion against Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace — for CIO and IT-buyer readers. CMEK, EU/Australia residency, and offline editing are the checklist items that decide bake-offs in regulated procurement; shipping them is Notion moving from "the tool a team adopts" to "the tool a bank's security review approves." The bank-pilot fact is what makes it a power story rather than a feature story. Not for consumer productivity press, note-taking-app review sites, or AI-tooling newsletters.
- **Why now:** The capability ships now, and the bank pilots are live now — that pairing is the whole news.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard product-and-customer news; the competitive framing extends it only if a rival or a bank comments.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, power elite plus conflict — the protagonist is the category's power balance, not the buyer or the compliance rulebook. The other angles take the regulated buyer's seat or the security-architecture seat.
- **Proof it needs:** confirmation of what "four of the five largest US banks now run pilots" means in writing — named banks or, if unnameable, the count's source, pilot scope, and seat numbers; whether the pilots predate or depend on CMEK; a competitive comparison the user can defend on which of these controls Microsoft and Google already offer and which they don't.
- **Facts it rests on:** CMEK via AWS KMS for enterprise; EU and Australia data residency added; four of the five largest US banks now run pilots; ~100M users.

**The bank that wouldn't let its staff use Notion is now piloting it**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A technology reporter at a banking or financial-services trade outlet who covers bank IT procurement, vendor risk, and shadow IT. The story is the buyer's, not the vendor's: what specifically blocked adoption before — key custody, residency, or offline access — and what changed in the vendor-risk questionnaire. This reporter's readers are the people who write those questionnaires. Not for general startup press or consumer tech desks.
- **Why now:** The pilots are running now and the controls that made them possible just shipped; the window closes once the story is told by someone else or the pilots go quiet.
- **Decay:** week — a buyer-side story survives past announcement day if a customer voice is available; without one it decays with the news.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, customer protagonist, and ladder lens zoomed to the bottom rung — one buyer, one blocker, one unlock. The counterposition angle is about the market; this one is about a procurement file.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one bank, insurer, or healthcare provider willing to be named or to speak on background through the reporter; the specific control that was the blocker; whether any pilot has converted to a paid deployment; what "unblocks regulated buyers" means in evidence rather than in the company's own words.
- **Facts it rests on:** the company says this unblocks regulated buyers in finance and healthcare that previously could not adopt; four of the five largest US banks now run pilots; CMEK via AWS KMS; EU and Australia data residency.

**Who holds the keys: what customer-managed encryption actually protects against**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A security reporter at an enterprise-security or cloud-infrastructure trade outlet covering key management and cloud data sovereignty. CMEK is widely marketed and narrowly understood; the reportable question is what customer-held keys in AWS KMS do and do not change — access during processing, subpoena response, insider access, and what happens when a customer revokes a key on a live workspace. A vendor shipping it to ~100M-user scale is a concrete peg for a piece the beat has been circling. Not for the business desk, not for product-launch roundups.
- **Why now:** The implementation is new and specific enough to report against; the underlying sovereignty debate is not time-pressured, so this runs on the reporter's schedule, not the user's.
- **Decay:** week — a context piece that stays usable while the launch is still current.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens zoomed up to the architecture question, with the news-values surprise angle underneath — the story is the control model, and the company is one example inside it. It does not need a bank, a competitor, or a buyer to work.
- **Proof it needs:** technical documentation of the KMS integration; a straight answer on whether Notion can read customer content while it is being processed, and what breaks when a key is revoked; whether residency covers backups, logs, telemetry, and any AI features; a named engineer who will take hostile technical questions.
- **Facts it rests on:** enterprise customers can hold their own encryption keys via AWS KMS; data residency options for the EU and Australia; ~100M users.

**A cloud-native workspace is rebuilding for the offline case it spent a decade dismissing**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A software-engineering or developer-tools reporter at a technical outlet covering local-first architecture and sync conflict resolution. The prevailing belief in SaaS has been that always-online is fine and offline is a legacy requirement; a cloud-era workspace shipping offline-first at ~100M-user scale is evidence against it — and the interesting part is the engineering, since sync-on-reconnect means conflict resolution, not caching. Not for business desks or enterprise IT press, who want the procurement story instead.
- **Why now:** Offline-first ships now. The architectural argument itself is not deadline-bound.
- **Decay:** week — the launch dates the peg; the local-first argument outlives it by a few days at most without a technical source.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — the only angle here where the fact cuts against a stated belief rather than confirming a direction. Protagonist is the architecture, not the buyer, the market, or the key model.
- **Proof it needs:** an engineering source who will explain the conflict-resolution model concretely; what happens to two people editing one block offline for a week; whether offline-first is available on all platforms or a subset; whether it was built for regulated buyers or for connectivity, since the pitch depends on which. Without a technical source this angle is unpitchable.
- **Facts it rests on:** offline editing works with no connection and syncs on reconnect; ~100M users; enterprise CMEK and residency shipped alongside it.

**Notion added sovereignty controls for the EU and Australia without raising Enterprise prices**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A SaaS business-model or enterprise-procurement reporter at a business outlet or subscription-economy newsletter. The pattern worth reporting is that compliance features are usually a paid tier — sovereignty as an upsell is standard practice across enterprise software — and here they arrive inside the existing Enterprise plan. Whether that is a land-grab against incumbents or a signal that these controls are becoming table stakes is a real, arguable question. Not for security reporters, and not for the trade press covering banks.
- **Why now:** The no-price-change decision is only news alongside the launch; it has no independent clock.
- **Decay:** 24hr — pricing decisions are news on announcement day and become background after.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the money as protagonist — the story is the commercial choice, not the technology or the customer. Weakest of the kept set: it needs a comparison the user must supply.
- **Proof it needs:** what competitors charge for equivalent key-management and residency controls, sourced from public pricing pages; an executive on record about why this stays in the base Enterprise tier; whether any of it is gated by seat minimum, contract length, or a separate SKU despite "no pricing change"; whether pilot-stage banks are paying anything.
- **Facts it rests on:** no pricing change for existing Enterprise plans; CMEK via AWS KMS; EU and Australia data residency; the company says this unblocks regulated buyers in finance and healthcare.

- "Notion transforms the future of secure collaboration" — `slop`.
- "Notion's offline-first mode brings seamless productivity anywhere" — `slop`.
- "Notion hits 100M users" — `no_why_now_but_required`. The user count is context in these facts, not an event. There is no milestone date, no growth rate, and no before-number; a bare number is not a story.
- "Why healthcare IT leaders should care about Notion's new compliance features" — `hallucinated_fact`. Pitching a healthcare trade on a vendor's own assertion is how a reporter learns to ignore the sender.
- "Notion's enterprise push shows AI-era workspaces must earn regulated trust" — `hallucinated_fact`. Nothing in these facts mentions AI. That framing is imported, not supplied.
- "Data residency arrives for European customers" — `duplicate`. Same lens and same protagonist as the sovereignty-controls angle, with weaker proof behind it; one story, not two.
- "What Notion's offline mode means for remote and hybrid work" — `no_journalist_shape`. Future-of-work is an audience, not a beat, and there is no evidence here about how anyone works.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Can you name a single bank? "Four of the five largest US banks now run pilots" is the strongest fact in this update and also the most fragile. Reporters will ask which banks, who told you, and whether the banks know you are saying it publicly. If none can be named and the count came from an internal sales dashboard, expect the claim to be cut from the story — and expect the reporter to trust the rest of the pitch less afterward.
- Is a pilot a customer? A pilot can mean a signed enterprise contract or forty people in one department on a trial. The gap between those two readings is the entire credibility of the finance angle. Decide what you will say when asked, before you are asked.
- Who unlocked whom? "The company says this unblocks regulated buyers" is your assertion, not a finding. Do you have one buyer who will confirm they were blocked and now are not? Without that, the strongest angles here thin out into a feature announcement with a good adjective.
- Can Notion read customer content? A security reporter will ask this in the first five minutes, and CMEK via a cloud KMS often does not mean what buyers assume. If the honest answer is "yes, during processing," you need to know that before you pitch the security beat — not during the interview.
- Does residency cover everything? Backups, logs, telemetry, support access, and any derived data. Partial residency described as residency is the kind of imprecision that becomes a correction.
- Is there any current news signal behind this? These facts contain none — no regulator action, no rule deadline, no competitor move. Every "why now" above rests on your own launch, which is a real but ordinary clock. If a live sovereignty or bank-IT story is running this week, the counterposition and security angles get materially stronger; without one, they are launch news.
- Are the offline and enterprise-security stories one announcement or two? They serve different beats and different reporters. Bundling them makes both softer, and no reporter has to cover the half they do not care about.
