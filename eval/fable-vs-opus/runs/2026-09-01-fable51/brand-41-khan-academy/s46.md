**Khan Academy is putting AI tutors in front of 1 million students. The study saying it works hasn't been peer-reviewed.**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** Education data reporter or investigative education reporter at a national education outlet or ed-policy trade publication. The hook is the gap between rollout speed and evidence quality — districts are signing on to expand to 1 million students based on a study whose methodology has not survived peer review. A reporter on this beat can ask what "extra weeks of learning" actually measures, who conducted the study, and what it would take to call this a real result. Not for general consumer tech press, AI hype outlets, or local news.
- **Why now:** Khan Academy is publishing first classroom results and simultaneously rolling out to 30 more districts — the results release and the expansion announcement arrive together, creating a before/after frame that gives an editor a concrete news hook.
- **Decay:** week — the results publication is the peg; the evidentiary debate carries forward into slower-burn analysis.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens — the protagonist is the quality of the evidence, not the product or the funding model. Different from the teacher angle (different user group) and the nonprofit model angle (different question entirely).
- **Proof it needs:** The actual study methodology and sample size; Khan Academy's precise definition of "extra weeks of learning" (which test, which baseline, what control conditions); an independent education researcher willing to comment on the study design on record; the publication venue and date for the internal results.
- **Facts it rests on:** Internal study found students who used Khanmigo regularly gained the equivalent of extra weeks of learning, by Khan's own measure; study is not yet peer-reviewed; rollout reaches an estimated 1 million additional students across 30 more US school districts.

**Khan Academy is offering AI tutoring to public school teachers for free. Here's who's actually paying.**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** EdTech business reporter or education finance reporter at an EdTech trade outlet, a philanthropy-focused publication, or a business press outlet covering nonprofit-versus-commercial dynamics in education technology. While VC-backed EdTech companies charge districts, a nonprofit is absorbing the cost through donor funding. The reporter's job is to find out whether that model holds at 1 million students and what happens to those students if it doesn't. Not for classroom-level education journalists, learning-science beat writers, or general AI business press.
- **Why now:** The 30-district expansion represents a meaningful jump in scale. The bigger the rollout, the sharper the sustainability question.
- **Decay:** week — the expansion news creates the peg; the funding sustainability angle carries beyond 24 hours.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, donor/funding model as protagonist — asks who bears the cost and what the model's limits are. Structurally different from the evidence angle (what the data shows) and the teacher angle (what the product does for a different user group).
- **Proof it needs:** Dollar amount of donor funding supporting Khanmigo; names of major donors or foundations involved; cost per student at current and projected scale; whether there is a revenue backstop or earned-income plan if donation levels fall; any public statements on sustainability beyond the current rollout.
- **Facts it rests on:** Khan Academy is a nonprofit; Khanmigo is offered to teachers for free; funding comes from donors; rollout reaches an estimated 1 million additional students.

**Schools are getting AI student tutors. Teachers are quietly getting something else: an AI lesson planner.**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** Education workforce reporter or teaching-practice reporter at a trade outlet covering teacher workload, professional development, or classroom labor. The public story about Khanmigo centers on student tutoring — but there is a second product embedded in the rollout: lesson-planning assistance for teachers. A reporter on this beat can tell the story through a teacher's experience — what gets handed off, what stays hard, what changes in the daily preparation routine. Not for student-outcome reporters, EdTech product reviewers, or general AI business press.
- **Why now:** The 30-district expansion brings more teachers into the product, making this a natural moment to report on teacher experience at scale.
- **Decay:** week — tied to the rollout news cycle, with the teacher-experience angle having a longer editorial shelf life than the announcement itself.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, teacher as protagonist — the beat is education workforce, not learning science or EdTech finance. The other angles look at the evidence, the money, and the critics; this one looks at what the product actually changes for the adults in the room.
- **Proof it needs:** At least one named teacher willing to describe the lesson-planning experience on record; specifics on which planning tasks the tool handles; time estimates or measurable workload change; whether teachers can customize or override suggestions.
- **Facts it rests on:** Khanmigo gives teachers lesson-planning help; rollout expands to 30 more US school districts.

**Schools are scaling up step-by-step AI tutors. Some educators worry the help is arriving before students learn to struggle.**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** Learning science or education psychology reporter at an education research publication, or a features reporter at a national education outlet covering pedagogy debates. The tension is built into the product design: Khanmigo gives students step-by-step guidance, but a body of research on productive failure and desirable difficulty argues that working through difficulty without immediate help is itself a learning mechanism. A reporter on this beat can use the rollout as the news hook and the pedagogical debate as the substance. Not for AI tech press, EdTech business reporters, or local news.
- **Why now:** The first results publication and the 1 million-student expansion raise the stakes on the pedagogical question — this is no longer a pilot.
- **Decay:** week — the results release provides the peg; the underlying pedagogy debate has a longer window.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — takes the product's core feature (step-by-step tutoring) and asks whether that feature is also a risk. Structurally different from the evidence angle (which is about research quality) and the funding angle (which is about business model). Different protagonist: the skeptical educator, not the study author or the donor.
- **Proof it needs:** Named educators or learning scientists willing to articulate the independence-at-risk critique on record; a specific mechanism (e.g., references to productive failure or desirable difficulty research); documentation of when Khanmigo withholds a direct answer versus provides a step; any signals in the internal study about unassisted task performance.
- **Facts it rests on:** Some educators question AI tutoring's effect on independent thinking; Khanmigo gives students step-by-step tutoring.

## Refused angles

- "Khanmigo is revolutionizing the way students learn" — `slop`. Press-release headline; no journalist would write it.
- "Khan Academy's AI proves the achievement gap can be closed with technology" — `hallucinated_fact`. Nothing in the supplied facts makes this claim. The study is internal and measures a different outcome.
- "A million more students now have access to world-class AI learning" — `slop`. "World-class" is a banned superlative; the framing is announcement copy, not a story.
- "This is the next chapter in the AI-in-education story" — `no_journalist_shape`. There is no specific sub-beat, no specific reporter type, and no reason this pitch would land differently from any other AI-in-education press release.

## Uncomfortable questions

1. The "extra weeks of learning" claim is the linchpin of the data angle — and without knowing the measure, the baseline, the sample size, and the control conditions, you cannot pitch it without exposing yourself to immediate fact-check scrutiny. What does the study actually say?
2. Is the internal study being published anywhere — a company report, a white paper, an education conference presentation? The publication venue determines how seriously reporters will treat it.
3. Do you have any named teachers willing to speak on record about the lesson-planning feature? The teacher angle is the most differentiated pitch in this set, and it needs at least one real voice to move from topic to story.
4. Who are the "some educators" questioning the effect on independent thinking? A named researcher or association position makes that angle pitchable; anonymous skepticism does not.
5. Who are the major donors funding the free rollout, and is there any public documentation of sustainability planning at 1 million students? Without this, the funding-mechanics angle stays thin.
