**An AI tutor's own pilot data: regular users gained the equivalent of extra weeks of learning**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education data or ed-tech reporter at an education trade outlet (think a K-12 / learning-research desk) who covers learning-outcome claims and is fluent in how to read them. The hook is the first published classroom result, with a number attached, from one of the largest AI-tutoring deployments. This reporter's instinct is to interrogate the methodology — internal study, not peer-reviewed, "by Khan's own measure" — which is exactly the honest version of this story. Not for general consumer-tech press chasing an "AI helps kids" headline that ignores the caveats.
- **Why now:** The results are being published alongside the 30-district rollout today, so the number is newly public and tied to a live distribution event.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the publication of results plus rollout is a same-day company-news event; the methodology debate can run a few days longer but the peg is the announcement.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens (scale, contextualized as "extra weeks of learning"). It is the only angle built primarily on the metric itself and on its limits, not on people, districts, or the broader debate.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual study — sample size, how many districts, the comparison group, what "regular use" means, and exactly what "weeks of learning" is measured against; a plain statement of who funded and ran the study; a timeline for peer review or external replication. Without the underlying numbers this angle is a press release.
- **Facts it rests on:** an internal study across pilot districts found students who used Khanmigo regularly gained the equivalent of extra weeks of learning by Khan's own measure; the study is not yet peer-reviewed; results are being published now alongside the rollout.

**Does an AI tutor build independent thinking or quietly replace it?**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education reporter or learning-science writer at a thoughtful education or policy outlet who covers pedagogy debates, not product launches. The prevailing optimism — AI tutoring boosts learning — meets a real, named counter-belief here: some educators question AI tutoring's effect on independent thinking. That tension, attached to a million-student rollout, is the story. Not for outlets that want a simple pro/anti AI culture-war framing.
- **Why now:** The expansion to 30 districts and roughly 1 million more students makes the "what does step-by-step help do to independent thinking" question newly concrete and at scale.
- **Decay:** week — this is a debate/trend angle; the rollout gives it a peg but the question outlives the news cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — it cuts against the learning-gains framing using a belief and a skeptic group both present in the facts. It is the only angle whose protagonist is the doubters, not Khan.
- **Proof it needs:** named educators or researchers willing to articulate the independent-thinking concern on record; whether the study measured anything about over-reliance, hint-seeking, or problem-solving stamina, or only outcome gains; Khan's own position on how step-by-step tutoring is designed to avoid spoon-feeding answers.
- **Facts it rests on:** Khanmigo gives students step-by-step tutoring; some educators question AI tutoring's effect on independent thinking; the rollout reaches an estimated 1 million additional students.

**A nonprofit is giving an AI tutor to teachers for free while the rest of ed-tech charges per seat**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** An ed-tech business or market reporter covering the economics of the AI-tutoring category — who pays, who profits, who gets locked out. The hook is the model: a donor-funded nonprofit handing the tool to teachers for free, set against a market where AI tutoring is typically a paid product. This reporter can test what "free, funded by donors" does to the category's pricing and access dynamics. Not for the learning-outcomes desk (that's the data angle) and not for general nonprofit/philanthropy puff coverage.
- **Why now:** The 30-district expansion is the moment the free-to-teachers model scales visibly, making the funding question timely.
- **Decay:** week — the business-model contrast is a context piece that the rollout pegs but does not exhaust in a day.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the money/category as protagonist — it is about the funding and access model, not the metric or the pedagogy debate.
- **Proof it needs:** who the donors are and how durable the funding is (one-time grants vs. sustained); the actual cost to run Khanmigo at this scale and whether "free" is sustainable past the donor money; any comparison to what paid competitors charge that the user can supply, since no competitor pricing is in the facts.
- **Facts it rests on:** Khan Academy is a nonprofit and offers Khanmigo to teachers for free, funded by donors; the rollout reaches an estimated 1 million additional students.

**Khanmigo lands in 30 more districts — what changes inside the classrooms that get it**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education reporter at a regional or K-12 trade outlet covering what new tools actually do for teachers and students on the ground. The entry point is the dual use — students get step-by-step tutoring, teachers get lesson-planning help — landing in named districts. This is the concrete, low-altitude rung: one district, one teacher, one before/after. Not for national business desks or AI-industry press, which want the scale or the money, not the classroom.
- **Why now:** The 30-district rollout is happening now, giving a reporter a fresh, locatable hook in specific communities.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the rollout event dates this; the on-the-ground color holds slightly longer if a district will host a reporter.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed down to the classroom — the protagonist is a teacher/district, distinct from the system-wide data, the debate, and the funding model.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named district and a teacher or administrator willing to describe what changed in lesson planning and tutoring; whether the lesson-planning help has any results behind it or only the student-tutoring side does; access for a reporter to observe or interview.
- **Facts it rests on:** the rollout reaches 30 more US school districts; Khanmigo gives students step-by-step tutoring and gives teachers lesson-planning help; an estimated 1 million additional students are reached.

**Refused angles**

- "Khan Academy's revolutionary AI tutor transforms education for a million students" — `slop`. Press-release superlatives ("revolutionary," "transforms"); no real beat.
- "Khanmigo is the future of personalized learning" — `slop`. AI-marketing framing, not a story a reporter would write.
- "Khanmigo proves AI tutoring closes the achievement gap" — `hallucinated_fact`. The facts say "extra weeks of learning by Khan's own measure," nothing about equity, achievement gaps, or which students benefited.
- "Khan Academy beats [competitor] in the AI-tutoring race" — `hallucinated_fact`. No competitor, no comparative metric, and no race is in the facts.
- "Why teachers everywhere are embracing AI lesson planning" — `no_journalist_shape`. "Everywhere" is unsupported; the teacher-facing lesson-planning feature has no results or named users behind it, so there's no defensible beat yet.
- "A million more students get an AI tutor — the scale story" — `duplicate`. The 1-million figure is real but on its own it's the same event as the rollout; it lives inside the data and customer-story angles rather than as a separate one.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- The headline number is "by Khan's own measure" and not peer-reviewed. Will you lead a pitch with it knowing a good education reporter will ask for the methodology immediately — and do you have that methodology to hand them?
- What does "regularly" mean, and what share of pilot students actually used Khanmigo regularly? If the gains only apply to a self-selected, motivated minority, the scale framing is misleading.
- Were the gains measured against a control group, or against the same students' prior trajectory? This single fact decides whether the data angle survives a reporter's first call.
- Who are the donors funding the free-to-teachers model, and is the funding durable enough that "free" survives the next budget cycle? The counterposition angle depends on it.
- Can you name a district, a teacher, or an educator-critic on record? Right now every angle except the bare data point needs a named human you have not yet supplied.
- The "independent thinking" concern is in your own fact block. Do you have a substantive answer to it, or will engaging the contrarian angle hand a skeptical reporter the lede?
