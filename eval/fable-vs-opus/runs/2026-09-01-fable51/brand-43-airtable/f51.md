**Airtable drops the "spreadsheet-database" pitch and rebuilds itself around AI agents**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** An enterprise-software reporter covering low-code and no-code tooling and workplace productivity platforms, at a business-tech outlet or an enterprise IT trade. The hook is not the feature list. It is that a company whose identity was "spreadsheet-database" is publicly abandoning that identity and asking to be judged as an app platform where agents do the work. This reporter can test whether that is a real category shift for internal tooling or a relabel. Not for consumer tech press, AI-research desks, general startup blogs, or local business reporters.
- **Why now:** The repositioning and the agent launch are the live event. The category question has a longer tail if Airtable can show enterprise teams actually running agents on their data.
- **Decay:** week. Launch news is a one-day event, but a repositioning of a widely known product supports a week-long trend piece.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up. The category is the protagonist, not the company's balance sheet or the operational risk of the agents. The other two kept angles drop to the money and to the buyer's control panel.
- **Proof it needs:** an on-record Airtable executive explaining why the old label is being retired; one enterprise team, named, that has moved an internal workflow to agents; any adoption figure for apps or agents, even a directional one; a clear definition of what "AI-native app platform" includes that a no-code builder did not.
- **Facts it rests on:** Airtable is shifting its messaging from "spreadsheet-database" to "AI-native app platform"; users can build internal apps and now deploy AI agents that read records and take actions; the company serves many enterprise teams.

**Airtable's agents can now update your records and draft your emails. It has not said how often they get it wrong.**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** An enterprise IT reporter covering agent governance, data access controls, and rollout risk, at a CIO-focused trade outlet or an IT-operations newsletter. The reader is the admin who has to decide whether to let software write to production records. That reader wants to know what the agents can change without a human, whether actions are logged and reversible, and what Airtable will say about error rates. The absence of disclosed reliability data is itself the story shape. Not for founder-profile desks, marketing press, or funding reporters.
- **Why now:** The agents are launching, so the question of what they are allowed to do lands while buyers are evaluating them.
- **Decay:** 24hr for the launch hook. The governance question stays open as long as reliability data is undisclosed, but the news reason to ask it is the launch itself.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the enterprise buyer as protagonist, plus the relevance news value. It is about the mechanics and risk of agents acting on company data, not about market positioning or valuation. This is the only angle where the missing reliability data is the point rather than a hole.
- **Proof it needs:** a written description of what actions agents can take autonomously versus with approval; whether actions are logged, attributable, and reversible; any reliability, accuracy, or error data Airtable is willing to share, or an on-record explanation of why it is not sharing any; an enterprise admin willing to describe their rollout rules.
- **Facts it rests on:** AI agents read records and take actions like updating statuses or drafting emails; agent-reliability data is not disclosed; the company serves many enterprise teams.

**Airtable's AI pivot is also a valuation story: private, $11B, and below its peak**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A private-markets or late-stage venture reporter at a financial or VC-focused outlet who tracks how software companies marked down from their peak try to earn their way back. The hook is the gap between the last mark and the peak, and whether an agent platform is the growth argument investors want to hear. This reporter can ask what the company is worth today, not what it says it is becoming. Not for product-review outlets, IT trades, or productivity columnists.
- **Why now:** The repositioning gives the reporter a reason to revisit the valuation now. Without a stated current mark or an investor on record, the peg is soft.
- **Decay:** week. Valuation and investor-thesis pieces run after the launch day if there is a real financial detail to anchor them.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, investor and money as protagonist. The story is whether the pivot changes the company's financial trajectory, not what the product does or what buyers must control.
- **Proof it needs:** the peak valuation figure and its date; whether $11B is still the current mark or a stale one; revenue or growth figures, or an investor on record on the agent thesis; any recent secondary transaction or tender that establishes a price.
- **Facts it rests on:** the company is privately held and was last valued at $11B, down from a higher peak; Airtable is repositioning as an AI app-building platform.

- "Airtable walks away from the label that made it famous" — `duplicate`. Same ladder-up lens and same protagonist as the category angle; the contrast is already that angle's lede.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Is the agent launch public as of today, or did it ship earlier? If it went out days ago, the 24-hour window on the governance angle is already closing and the pitch needs a new reason.
- Is there one enterprise customer willing to be named as running agents on live records? Without one, the category angle is a messaging claim, not a market claim.
- What can an agent do without a human approving it, and can it be undone? If you cannot answer that in two sentences, the enterprise IT angle turns against you.
- Why is reliability data undisclosed? If it is because it does not exist yet, say that internally before a reporter asks.
- What was the peak valuation, and is $11B still the current mark? A reporter on the money angle will find the number; better to decide now what you will say about it.
- Who is on record? None of the three angles survives contact with a reporter without a named executive.
