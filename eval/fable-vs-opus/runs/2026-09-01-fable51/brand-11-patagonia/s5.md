**Patagonia's Worn Wear hits 500,000 resold items as repair goes nationwide**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail or circular-economy trade reporter — think a resale/secondhand-retail beat or a sustainable-business vertical at a business outlet — who covers whether repair-and-resale is graduating from boutique perk to standard retail infrastructure. The story isn't "Patagonia did a nice thing," it's whether a national chain putting free repair drop-off in 70+ stores plus mail-in is evidence the category itself has matured. Not for consumer-tech press or general lifestyle desks chasing a single feel-good item — this is an infrastructure/business-model story.
- **Why now:** The nationwide store rollout and mail-in launch are happening now, and the 500,000-item resale figure gives the "at scale" claim a hard number.
- **Decay:** week — a trend/context piece, not spot news; worth the time to gather comparative data.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — treats the rollout as evidence for the broader resale/repair category rather than a story about Patagonia specifically. Distinct from the business-model angle below, which stays inside Patagonia's own strategic logic rather than the market.
- **Proof it needs:** comparable repair/resale programs (free basic repair, this footprint) at other retailers; how "70+ stores" compares to Patagonia's total US store count; whether the 500,000-item figure is all-time or a defined window.
- **Facts it rests on:** repair drop-off now available at 70+ US stores plus mail-in nationwide; basic repairs free, complex ones charged at cost; Worn Wear has resold over 500,000 used items.

**Patagonia expands a program built to sell you less clothing**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A business-strategy or retail-economics reporter who covers corporate incentive structures — the kind who asks why a company that sells clothes would build free infrastructure that keeps people from buying more. Prevailing belief: retail growth requires maximizing new-unit sales, and sustainability programs are usually margin-safe marketing that doesn't touch revenue. The facts here — free basic repairs, and no new apparel line announced alongside this launch — are evidence against that belief. Not for lifestyle desks chasing a feel-good sustainability story, and not for sustainability trade press that will take the mission framing at face value without pressing on the business logic.
- **Why now:** The nationwide expansion is what makes the business-model question timely; without it, this is just background on stated values.
- **Decay:** week — needs the reporter to press for strategic or financial reasoning, which takes longer than a same-day writeup.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — names a real prevailing belief and uses supplied facts against it. Distinct from the trend angle above (market evidence, not strategic contradiction) and from the disclosure angle below (what Patagonia won't say, not what it's choosing to do).
- **Proof it needs:** any on-record Patagonia comment on how repair affects new-apparel sales; whether internal targets exist for repair/resale as a share of business; analyst comment on whether repair programs measurably reduce repeat purchases.
- **Facts it rests on:** basic repairs free, complex ones charged at cost; no new apparel line announced alongside this launch; Patagonia is privately held and directs profits to environmental causes.

**How to get Patagonia gear repaired for free now that it's in every store**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer or lifestyle reporter doing service journalism — the shopping/style desk explainer writer, or a consumer-facing sustainability outlet — whose readers own Patagonia gear and want the mechanics: drop-off vs. mail-in, what's free vs. what's charged, before their next torn jacket goes in the donation pile. Not for financial or B2B trade press, who won't care about individual repair logistics.
- **Why now:** The drop-off network and mail-in option are new as of this launch, so the "here's how it works" piece is only accurate starting now.
- **Decay:** 24hr — rides directly on the launch; standard company-news pacing.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, customer protagonist, zoomed to the concrete/individual rung — one reader's repair experience, not the market or the strategy. Distinct from the trend and business-model angles, which stay at company or market altitude.
- **Proof it needs:** a named customer willing to describe an actual repair, ideally before/after; where the line sits between "basic" (free) and "complex" (charged) repairs, since the update doesn't define it; typical mail-in turnaround time.
- **Facts it rests on:** customers can drop off any Patagonia item at 70+ US stores or mail it in; basic repairs free, complex ones charged at cost.

**Patagonia won't say how big its resale business actually is**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail-financial or data reporter — apparel-sector trade press or a business desk's retail-earnings beat — interested in sizing the resale market rather than taking the press milestone at face value. The hook is the gap: Patagonia will publicize a round-number milestone (500,000 items) but won't say what share of revenue that represents, which is exactly the kind of withheld figure a financial reporter would push on. Not for consumer or lifestyle desks, who won't find a withheld percentage compelling on its own.
- **Why now:** The milestone announcement is what raises the question in the first place.
- **Decay:** week — getting comment on withheld figures, or finding a benchmark to estimate against, takes longer than a same-day story.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, analyst protagonist — someone trying to size a business Patagonia won't size itself. Distinct from the business-model angle above, which is about strategic intent, not the missing number.
- **Proof it needs:** any public benchmark for resale-as-share-of-revenue at comparable apparel or outdoor retailers; an on-record Patagonia comment on why the figure isn't disclosed; confirmation of whether Patagonia, as a private company, discloses any revenue figures at all as a baseline.
- **Facts it rests on:** the company will not say what share of revenue resale represents; Worn Wear has resold over 500,000 used items; Patagonia is privately held and directs profits to environmental causes.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Will Patagonia produce even one named customer with a repair or resale story, or does the customer-story angle stay hypothetical?
- Why won't the company share what share of revenue resale represents — confirm the reasoning before a reporter turns the refusal itself into the story.
- Is "no new apparel line announced" a deliberate signal worth a spokesperson confirming, or just the absence of unrelated news? The business-model angle shouldn't assume restraint that hasn't been confirmed.
- What exactly separates a "basic" (free) repair from a "complex" (charged) one? Without a definition, the service-journalism angle risks over-promising "free" to readers.
