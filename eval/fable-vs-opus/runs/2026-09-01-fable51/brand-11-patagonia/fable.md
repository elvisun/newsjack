- **Why a journalist cares:** A data or business reporter at a retail or resale trade outlet covering the economics of branded recommerce. The 500,000-item figure is the first hard number here, and the company's refusal to disclose resale's revenue share is part of the story — it lets the reporter test whether branded resale is a business or a marketing program. Not for consumer-lifestyle press, gear-review outlets, or general sustainability blogs that won't interrogate the number.
- **Why now:** The number was released alongside today's repair expansion, which gives it a fresh peg; the analytical question behind it holds for about a week.
- **Decay:** week — the milestone rides the announcement, but a data story interrogating it can run days later.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, scale plus a disclosure gap. The other angles are about the repair expansion; this one is about the resale number and what the company won't say about it.
- **Proof it needs:** time frame for the 500,000 figure (cumulative since when?); average resale price or any unit economics Patagonia will put on record; an on-record answer — or refusal — to the revenue-share question.
- **Facts it rests on:** Worn Wear has resold over 500,000 used items; the company will not say what share of revenue resale represents.

**Free repairs at every store cut against how apparel retail makes money**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail business reporter covering brand strategy and unit economics. The prevailing model in apparel is volume — growth comes from selling new units, and repairs are a cost center retailers minimize. Patagonia is doing the opposite: building nationwide infrastructure to keep already-sold items out of the replacement cycle, with basic repairs free. The announcement notably contains no new apparel line. Not for sustainable-fashion advocacy outlets (they'll just cheer it) and not for electronics right-to-repair press — this is textiles.
- **Why now:** The expansion to all 70+ US stores plus nationwide mail-in is announced now; the contrarian framing decays with the announcement.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard company-news cycle for the announcement itself.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — the story is the business logic the move defies, not the program mechanics or the resale number.
- **Proof it needs:** what "basic" versus "complex, charged at cost" actually covers; any number on repair volume or repair cost per item; a company spokesperson willing to defend the economics on record rather than the values.
- **Facts it rests on:** repairs available at 70+ US stores plus nationwide mail-in; basic repairs are free, complex ones charged at cost; no new apparel line announced.

**Only a company with no shareholders to please could give repairs away**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A corporate-governance or business-of-fashion reporter covering ownership structure and stakeholder capitalism. The money is the protagonist: Patagonia is privately held and directs profits to environmental causes, which is plausibly what makes a free nationwide repair program survivable — there's no quarterly earnings call where a margin-eating cost center gets killed. The reporter can ask whether any public apparel company could copy this. Not for product or gear desks, and not for outlets that already ran the ownership story without a new development to hang it on.
- **Why now:** The repair expansion is a concrete, current test case of what the ownership structure permits; without it this is an evergreen think piece.
- **Decay:** week — the structural argument outlives the news day but needs the expansion as its peg.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, owner/money protagonist — the ownership structure is the story, not the repair program or the consumer benefit. The contrarian angle says "this defies retail logic"; this one explains the mechanism that makes defying it possible.
- **Proof it needs:** an on-record company answer on how repair costs are absorbed; the refused revenue-share question asked again in this context; any historical cost figure for the repair program the company will share.
- **Facts it rests on:** Patagonia is privately held and directs profits to environmental causes; basic repairs are free, complex ones charged at cost; the program now spans 70+ US stores plus mail-in.

**Patagonia is building reverse logistics for clothing at national scale**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail-operations or supply-chain trade reporter covering reverse logistics and store operations. Most apparel reverse logistics is returns processing; a network that takes any item of the brand, in any store or by mail, triages it into free/at-cost/resale, and moves it through repair is operationally unusual. The reporter's question is how it actually works: staffing, triage, turnaround, where repairs physically happen. Not for general business desks or consumer press — this is an operations story.
- **Why now:** The nationwide mail-in launch is the new operational fact; before this, the operational footprint was smaller.
- **Decay:** week — operations trades run process stories on a slower clock than news desks, but the launch peg fades within the week.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed down to the machinery. The other angles are about money, business logic, and milestones; this one is about how the system physically runs.
- **Proof it needs:** where repairs are performed (in-store, regional hubs, central facility); expected turnaround times; repair-technician headcount or training; how items get routed between repair and the resale platform.
- **Facts it rests on:** customers can drop off any Patagonia item at 70+ US stores or mail it in; basic repairs free, complex at cost; resale platform Worn Wear operates alongside the repair program.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Why won't the company say what share of revenue resale represents? Every business reporter on the data angle will ask, and "we don't disclose" becomes the lede if there's no better answer ready.
- What was the repair program before this announcement? If most stores already took repairs, "all US stores" is an incremental expansion dressed as a launch, and the news desks will notice.
- Over what period were the 500,000 items resold? Cumulative-since-launch and last-twelve-months are very different stories.
- Can anyone put a number on repairs — volume, cost per repair, turnaround? Without at least one operational figure, the strongest two angles run on assertion.
