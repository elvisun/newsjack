**Plaid offers merchants a flat fee to route payments around the card networks**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A payments-industry reporter at a fintech or payments trade outlet who covers acceptance costs and the power balance between card networks and challengers. The story is the pricing model, not the product: a flat per-transaction fee positioned directly against percentage-based interchange. That reporter can ask the card networks and acquirers for a response and has a conflict to report. Not for consumer personal-finance desks, general startup blogs, or developer-tools press.
- **Why now:** The launch is live as of 2026-06-09. Without a named merchant or a network response, the window is the announcement cycle.
- **Decay:** 24hr. Standard launch news. It extends to a week only if a card network, acquirer, or merchant responds on record.
- **What makes it distinct:** Conflict lens, with the card networks as the counterparty. This is the only angle where the incumbents are the story. The FedNow angle is about the rail and the data angle is about merchant math.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual flat fee, stated as a number or a schedule; a named merchant willing to say why they chose it over cards; Plaid's own definition of "large transactions"; a plan for what you say when the reporter asks the networks for comment, because they will.
- **Facts it rests on:** merchants pay a flat per-transaction fee instead of card interchange; the product bypasses card networks; Plaid says early merchants see fees up to 70% lower than cards on large transactions.

**FedNow gets a merchant checkout use case as Plaid puts bank-to-bank payments in front of shoppers**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A payments-infrastructure or banking-technology reporter at a banking trade outlet or real-time-payments newsletter who tracks adoption of the Fed's instant rail. Their running question is which commercial products actually move volume onto FedNow. A merchant-facing product from a company with 12,000 institution connections is a data point in that story, and the reporter will want to know how much of that reach is FedNow-reachable today. Not for retail-operations press, consumer press, or reporters who cover card rewards.
- **Why now:** The launch is the peg. The facts describe FedNow as new, which gives the adoption story a live thread, but no current FedNow news signal was supplied, so do not claim one.
- **Decay:** week. Rail-adoption pieces run on a trend clock, not a launch clock, if Plaid can supply the technical detail.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the rail as protagonist. The counterposition angle is about who loses; this is about which public infrastructure wins and how a private network plugs into it.
- **Proof it needs:** a technical description of which leg of the transaction runs on FedNow and what happens when the payer's bank is not a FedNow participant; how many of the 12,000 connected institutions can receive or send on FedNow today; an on-record engineering or product lead who can explain settlement timing; confirmation of whether instant bank verification and the payment itself use the same rail.
- **Facts it rests on:** the product uses instant bank verification and the new FedNow rails; Plaid connects to over 12,000 financial institutions; the product bypasses card networks.

**How big does a purchase have to be before Pay by Bank beats a card?**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A data or analysis reporter at a merchant-payments or retail-operations trade outlet who covers cost of acceptance. The "up to 70% lower on large transactions" claim is a vendor headline; the story underneath it is the breakeven point. A flat fee is worse than a percentage on small tickets and better on large ones, so the crossover ticket size decides which merchants should care at all. That reporter can build a chart from the fee schedule and tell merchants where they land. Not for general fintech coverage, funding press, or anyone who will not run numbers.
- **Why now:** The launch puts the fee claim in public. The claim itself has no clock; the math is useful for as long as the pricing holds.
- **Decay:** week. Data and context pieces run after the launch day if the numbers are real and reproducible.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, relationship and variation sub-lenses: savings as a function of ticket size, and which merchant cohorts win or lose. The other two angles do not touch the merchant's own economics.
- **Proof it needs:** the flat fee as a number; the sample behind "up to 70%" including how many merchants, what ticket sizes, and over what period; the card cost each merchant was actually paying before, since "70% lower than cards" depends entirely on the comparison rate; a merchant willing to share before-and-after figures on record. Without the fee schedule this angle cannot be pitched.
- **Facts it rests on:** merchants pay a flat per-transaction fee instead of card interchange; Plaid says early merchants see fees up to 70% lower than cards on large transactions; no named launch merchant has gone on record.

**Refused angles**

- "One retailer cut its payment fees 70% with Pay by Bank" (customer-story) — `hallucinated_fact`. No merchant is on record, so there is no customer to build a customer story around. Bring one and this becomes the strongest angle in the set.
- "Plaid turns 12,000 bank connections into a payments network" (category-creation) — `duplicate`. Same protagonist and same conflict as the counterposition angle. The 12,000 figure is context for that angle, not a separate story.
- "Pay by Bank shows US consumers are ready to leave cards behind" (contrarian) — `hallucinated_fact`. A contrarian angle needs evidence against a prevailing belief. The facts contain no adoption, volume, or consumer data.
- "What shoppers give up when a payment skips the card networks" (defensive-comment) — `hallucinated_fact`. A real reporter will ask about disputes and refunds, but the facts say nothing about how Pay by Bank handles them. This is a question you must answer, not an angle you can pitch.
- "What Pay by Bank means for Plaid's valuation" (funding-mechanics) — `no_journalist_shape`. The only money fact is that Plaid is privately held. No round, no investor, no financials.
- "Plaid launches a seamless new way to pay" — `slop`. A press-release sentence, not a story.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Why is no launch merchant on record? A payments launch with no named customer reads as a pilot, and every reporter above will ask.
- What is the flat fee, in dollars? "Up to 70% lower on large transactions" is unpitchable until a reporter can compute the breakeven ticket size and see it is not a cherry-picked comparison.
- What does "large transactions" mean in the 70% claim, and what was the card rate those merchants were paying? The number is only as strong as the baseline.
- What happens on a disputed or fraudulent transaction? Bypassing the card networks means the reporter will ask what replaces card-network dispute handling. Have the answer before the first pitch goes out.
- How many of the 12,000 connected institutions can actually complete a FedNow payment today? If the honest answer is a fraction, the reach figure is a connectivity number, not a payments number, and the FedNow angle should be pitched that way.
- Interchange is revenue for institutions on the other side of those connections. Has any bank partner said anything on record about a product that routes around it?
- No current news signal was supplied. If there is a live interchange or FedNow story this week, the counterposition angle could move from 24hr to something sharper. Do not assume one exists.
