**Substack is no longer a newsletter platform. Now it has to prove it's a media company.**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A media reporter at a thinky business or media-criticism outlet covering independent media's structural evolution. The news isn't "Substack added video." The news is that a company whose entire identity was text-first subscription publishing is now competing across every major media format at once — newsletters, podcasts, video, and an algorithmic feed. The protagonist is the category, not the company. Not for tech product reporters covering feature releases, consumer lifestyle press, or VC trade coverage.
- **Why now:** The native video launch is live today; the "media app" strategic framing is the company's own public positioning.
- **Decay:** week — the launch is a 24hr event, but the category-shift question can support a longer trend angle through the week.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up. Every other kept angle drops to a specific protagonist — the business model, the incumbents, the prevailing data narrative, the creator operator. This one makes "what is independent media becoming?" the center of the story.
- **Proof it needs:** A media analyst or independent media researcher willing to characterize what Substack's multi-format move signals about where the category is heading; evidence of whether Substack's writers are actually requesting or adopting video, or whether the feature is supply-side only; a creator willing to describe their multi-format strategy in concrete terms.
- **Facts it rests on:** Substack launching native video; writers can record, host, and monetize video and podcasts directly; company has adopted a "media app" strategy with a TikTok-style feed; over 4 million paid subscriptions across all publications.

**YouTube runs on ads. Substack's video play bets that creators would rather own their audience.**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A platform-competition reporter at a tech outlet covering the creator economy and video markets. The structural contrast is the hook: YouTube and TikTok monetize through algorithmic distribution and advertising; Substack charges subscribers and offers creator-controlled audiences with a fixed revenue split. By entering video, Substack is proposing a different economic model for video, not just another video surface. Not for general consumer tech reporters, not for creator lifestyle coverage, not for outlets that don't cover platform economics.
- **Why now:** The video launch is live today; the broader debate over creator monetization models is active.
- **Decay:** week — platform economics analysis can live past the launch announcement.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, competitor protagonist. The category angle makes independent media the story; this one makes the head-to-head with incumbent video platforms the story. Different protagonist (the incumbents), different story (which economic model for video wins among creators who already have paying audiences).
- **Proof it needs:** A creator currently earning revenue on both YouTube and Substack who can speak on record about the tradeoff; or a media economist who can characterize the subscription-versus-ads model difference in concrete terms for creators at various audience sizes; whether any Substack creators already have substantial video audiences they built elsewhere.
- **Facts it rests on:** Substack launching native video; TikTok-style feed; 10% cut of subscription revenue; over 4 million paid subscriptions; writers can record, host, and monetize video directly.

**$400 million in creator earnings is an inconvenient number for the subscription fatigue narrative**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A media or digital-economics reporter at a publication covering the newsletter, subscription media, or creator economy space — specifically one who has covered or cited the subscription fatigue discourse of the past few years. The prevailing belief, widely published since 2022–2023, is that audiences are pulling back from paid subscriptions across digital media. A single platform disclosing that its top creators collectively earned over $400M last year, with 4 million paid subscriptions on record, is a specific named data point that cuts against that thesis. Not for tech product reporters focused on features, not for general consumer tech outlets.
- **Why now:** The $400M figure is being disclosed in conjunction with today's announcement; subscription fatigue has been a durable ongoing discourse.
- **Decay:** week — the earnings figure is the peg; the contrarian thesis doesn't expire this week but goes effectively evergreen within a month.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens. The protagonist is the prevailing narrative, and the company update is evidence against it. None of the other angles make the subscription fatigue discourse the story. The platform competition angle (angle 2) is about model economics between platforms; this angle is about whether paid subscriptions at scale are real.
- **Proof it needs:** Documentation that "subscription fatigue" is a widely published belief — the reporter likely has this; independent context on how $400M in creator earnings compares to prior disclosed figures from Substack or to comparable platforms (Patreon, Ghost); verification that this figure isn't cherry-picked framing — what year, which creators count as "top," how the figure was calculated.
- **Facts it rests on:** Top creators collectively earned over $400M last year; over 4 million paid subscriptions across all publications; 10% cut of subscription revenue.

Different protagonist, different story.

**Substack wants writers to post video. Whether that helps them or Substack more is the real question.**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A creator economy reporter at a trade outlet or newsletter covering tools and strategies for working independent creators. The category angle asks what independent media is becoming; this angle asks what a specific writer with 10,000 paid subscribers should actually do Tuesday morning.

## Uncomfortable questions

- What exactly counts as "top creators" in the $400M figure? Is this the top 10 earners, the top 100, the top 1,000? Without knowing the denominator, the number floats — a journalist will ask immediately, and if you can't answer, the contrarian and funding-mechanics angles both weaken.
- How public were the funding struggles? Is there a specific reported round, a shortfall on record, or a named source? "Has had public funding struggles" is your phrasing, not a citation — and a business reporter will want the reference before contextualizing the current disclosure.
- Does the TikTok-style feed surface content to non-subscribers algorithmically, or only within followed publications? That distinction is meaningful: discovery-to-conversion is a different product than subscription-only feed. The counterposition and creator-story angles rest on this.
- Are any of Substack's 4 million paid subscribers new since the last public figure, or is this the same number restated? The contrarian angle is stronger if there's a growth vector; it's weaker if the subscription count has been flat.
- Are any Substack creators willing to go on record about the video feature — either adopting it or declining it? Without a named creator voice, the customer-story angle and the counterposition angle both rely on inference rather than reporting.
