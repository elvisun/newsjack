**Substack is done being a newsletter company**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A platforms reporter at a media-business outlet or a creator-economy newsletter who covers how distribution platforms change shape. The hook is not the video feature itself. It is that a company built on paid text subscriptions now records, hosts, and monetizes video and podcasts, and runs a TikTok-style feed. That reporter can ask whether "newsletter platform" still describes Substack at all, and what it now competes with. Not for consumer gadget press, product-review outlets, or general startup blogs that will just reprint the feature list.
- **Why now:** Native video launches today. The feed strategy was already visible; video is the piece that makes the category shift hard to argue with.
- **Decay:** week. The launch is a 24-hour event, but the "what is Substack now" question holds for a week if the company can speak to strategy on record.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up. The category is the protagonist. The other kept angles drop down to the money, to one creator, and to the writers who may lose out.
- **Proof it needs:** an on-record statement of what the media-app strategy is meant to become; whether video subscriptions carry the same 10% cut as text; how Substack defines the competitive set now that it hosts video (the competitor take rates a reporter will compare against must be supplied, not guessed).
- **Facts it rests on:** writers can record, host, and monetize video and podcasts directly; the company has leaned into a media-app strategy with a TikTok-style feed; the platform takes a 10% cut of subscription revenue.

**What Substack's $400M creator number says about Substack's own revenue**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A media-business or markets reporter who covers the economics of private platform companies, especially ones with a public funding history. Substack has disclosed a creator payout and a take rate on the same day. A reporter will multiply them. The story is whether the implied company revenue supports the media-app expansion, and what the video launch changes about unit economics for a company that has had public funding struggles. Not for creator-tips newsletters, feature-review outlets, or local business desks.
- **Why now:** The $400M figure and the video launch land together, and the funding-struggle story is already public. This is the next chapter of a story the press is already telling.
- **Decay:** week. The numbers stay reportable after launch day if the company will speak to them.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, money as protagonist, plus the follow-up news value. It is about Substack's business, not its product or its creators.
- **Proof it needs:** whether $400M is gross subscription revenue before the 10% cut or net to creators; whether the figure includes anything other than subscription revenue; whether video introduces a different take rate or hosting costs; a spokesperson prepared to answer runway and profitability questions, because the math invites them.
- **Facts it rests on:** top creators collectively earned over $400M last year; the platform takes a 10% cut of subscription revenue; Substack is privately held and has had public funding struggles; over 4 million paid subscriptions across all publications.

- **Why a journalist cares:** A podcast-industry or creator-business trade reporter who covers hosting, monetization, and where independent creators park their audio and video. The concrete question is what a writer who already publishes a podcast or video elsewhere gains by consolidating on Substack, and what the 10% cut costs them against their current setup. Not for general media-strategy columnists or tech press that will only care about the platform narrative.
- **Why now:** The feature is live today, so a creator's first move onto it is a story with a date on it.
- **Decay:** week. A named creator's migration reads fine in the week after launch; it reads stale after that.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the creator as protagonist, at the bottom rung of the ladder. One named person, one concrete workflow, one number they will share.
- **Proof it needs:** a named Substack writer willing to describe moving or launching video or podcasts on the platform, including their prior hosting setup and what the 10% cut compares to; that creator's own earnings or subscriber figures, because the company will not supply individual numbers.
- **Facts it rests on:** writers can record, host, and monetize video and podcasts directly; the platform takes a 10% cut of subscription revenue; individual creator earnings beyond the aggregate are not disclosed.

**Substack's video feed raises a question for writers who never wanted to be on camera**

- **Story type:** defensive-comment
- **Why a journalist cares:** A media-industry reporter who covers independent journalism and the working conditions of writers. The feed plus native video creates an obvious question: does a text-only writer get the same distribution as a creator posting video into a TikTok-style feed? Reporters will write this whether or not Substack pitches it. The angle exists so the company answers it well on day one instead of badly on day three. Not for creator-economy hype outlets or product press; it will read as bad news to a beat that only wants launch coverage.
- **Why now:** The feed already exists and video launches today. The concern becomes concrete the moment writers see video in their subscribers' feeds.
- **Decay:** week. The question is sharpest during launch week, then becomes a slower-burning trend piece.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, the bad-news side of the same fact. The protagonist is the text-first writer who may lose, not the creator who gains. Angle three is about a creator who benefits; this is the mirror.
- **Proof it needs:** a clear on-record statement of whether text and video are ranked the same way in the feed; any evidence that text-only publications have not lost discovery since the feed launched; a spokesperson prepared for "are you pushing writers toward video" without deflecting.
- **Facts it rests on:** the company has leaned into a media-app strategy with a TikTok-style feed; writers can now record, host, and monetize video and podcasts directly; over 4 million paid subscriptions across all publications.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Is $400M gross subscription revenue before the 10% cut, or what creators actually took home? Reporters will do the math either way, and the two readings imply different company revenue.
- How many creators make up "top creators," and what is the median? "Not disclosed" will become the story if the answer is not ready.
- Does video carry the same 10% cut, and who pays for hosting and bandwidth? If the economics differ, the take-rate story changes.
- Are text posts and video ranked the same way in the feed? A vague answer confirms the writers' fear.
- Was $400M up or down from the prior year? Without that, the number cannot support a change angle and reporters will assume the worse reading.
- Is there a named creator willing to speak about moving video or podcasts onto the platform? Without one, the customer-story angle does not exist.
- Given the public funding history, is a spokesperson prepared to answer runway and profitability questions on launch day? The disclosures invite them.
