**Substack says its top creators split more than $400M last year — and won't say how the split works**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A creator-economy reporter at a media-business trade outlet or subscription-economy newsletter who covers platform payout disclosure — the kind who has previously pulled apart YouTube, Patreon and Spotify payout claims. The hook is the shape of the number, not its size: "top creators collectively" is a self-selected numerator with an undisclosed denominator, and Substack is simultaneously claiming 4 million paid subscriptions. A reporter can do arithmetic on that publicly. Not for consumer tech gadget desks, product-review writers, or general startup press who will just repeat the figure.
- **Why now:** The $400M figure is being put into circulation alongside the video launch today, which is the moment it's checkable and the moment the company is obliged to answer questions about it.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the number is only interrogable while it's live announcement material; after that it becomes an uncontested stat everyone cites.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens (scale, contextualized) crossed with the "so what" gate. Every other angle here treats the launch as the event; this one treats the disclosure itself as the event and makes Substack's own accounting the subject.
- **Proof it needs:** the definition of "top creators" — how many publications, what threshold; whether $400M is gross subscriber spend or net of the 10% cut, Stripe fees and refunds; the time period "last year" refers to (calendar 2025 or trailing twelve months); how the 4M paid subscriptions figure relates to that revenue; whether any of it has been audited or is company-stated only. Without the numerator/denominator, this angle cannot be pitched — it becomes the thing it's criticizing.
- **Facts it rests on:** top creators collectively earned over $400M last year; 10% cut of subscription revenue; over 4 million paid subscriptions across all publications; individual creator earnings beyond the aggregate not disclosed.

**A writing platform is now asking writers to become video producers**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A media-industry reporter covering the working conditions of independent writers — freelance-economy and press-labour desks, or a newsletter about the business of being a writer. The protagonist is the working newsletter writer, not Substack: native video and podcast hosting changes what the platform expects of them, and the people who chose Substack precisely because it was text now face a feed that rewards something they didn't sign up to make. Not for product-launch desks, video-tech trade press, or anyone who'd frame this as a feature story.
- **Why now:** The video tools ship now, so the shift in what the platform asks of its writers is a live, dated change rather than a general grievance.
- **Decay:** week — the launch dates it, but the labour question stays reportable for as long as writers are adjusting.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the creator as protagonist — the same launch, but the story is what it costs the people on the platform, not what it gains the platform. Distinct from the payout angle, which is about money, and from the strategy angle, which is about the company.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named Substack writer willing to describe the pressure to produce video on the record; whether video is required for feed distribution or optional; whether existing text-only publications lose reach; what the recording and hosting tools actually do and what they don't. A reporter will want writers on both sides — one who welcomes it, one who resents it.
- **Facts it rests on:** Substack is launching native video; writers can record, host and monetise video and podcasts directly; the platform has leaned into a media-app strategy with a TikTok-style feed.

**Substack has stopped competing with Mailchimp and started competing with YouTube**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A media-strategy reporter at a business outlet or media-analysis newsletter covering how platforms redefine their own category — the beat that tracks companies changing which market they're in and therefore which incumbents they have to beat. Native video plus a TikTok-style feed plus podcast monetisation is not a feature set for an email newsletter tool; it's the feature set of a creator video platform, where the 10% take rate becomes the pitch against much larger competitors. Not for email-marketing trade press, and not for reporters who cover Substack as a writers' story.
- **Why now:** Video shipping today is the moment the repositioning stops being inferred from the feed and becomes explicit product.
- **Decay:** week — the category argument outlives the launch cycle but needs the launch as its peg.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — the market Substack is now in is the story, not the launch or the writers. Different altitude and different protagonist from every other kept angle.
- **Proof it needs:** what the video product actually does versus a general-purpose host; whether the 10% cut applies identically to video revenue; whether the feed algorithmically favours video; a Substack executive on record about which competitors they think they're facing now. Comparative take-rate data for competing platforms must come from a real source, not assumption.
- **Facts it rests on:** launching native video; writers can record, host and monetise video and podcasts directly; 10% cut of subscription revenue; the media-app strategy with a TikTok-style feed.

**A company with funding problems is building a video platform**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A private-markets or startup-finance reporter covering the cost structures of venture-backed consumer platforms — someone who reads a product launch as a spending decision. Video hosting is one of the most expensive infrastructure commitments a platform can make, and this one is being made by a private company with a publicly rocky funding history and a 10% take on a 4-million-subscription base. The reportable question is what pays for the bandwidth. Not for product press, creator-economy cheerleaders, or consumer tech desks.
- **Why now:** The launch forces the cost question into the present; without it this is speculation about a private company's balance sheet.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the finance framing only rides on the announcement news cycle unless a reporter gets real numbers.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the money as protagonist, plus the news-values conflict/bad-news pairing — the same launch read as a liability rather than an achievement. The category angle asks what market this is; this one asks who pays for it.
- **Proof it needs:** the specifics of the funding struggles — which round, when, what terms, and a source for it, because "public funding struggles" is not itself a citable fact; current runway or profitability status; whether video is self-hosted or on a third-party CDN; whether the 10% take rate changes for video. This angle is unpitchable without documented funding history. Do not imply distress you cannot source.
- **Facts it rests on:** Substack is privately held and has had public funding struggles; launching native video with direct hosting; 10% cut of subscription revenue; over 4 million paid subscriptions.
