**Wise routes cross-border business payments around correspondent banking**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A payments-infrastructure reporter at a fintech or banking trade outlet who covers correspondent banking, SWIFT, and nostro/vostro economics. The story is not "fintech does crypto" — it is a listed payments company with £30B a quarter in volume telling its business customers they can bypass the bank-to-bank rail for part of that flow. That reporter can ask the banks what share of their cross-border fee income is exposed. Not for crypto/token press, consumer money-saving desks, or general startup coverage.
- **Why now:** The launch is live today, and correspondent banking's cost structure is the thing the launch is explicitly positioned against.
- **Decay:** 24hr — a product launch is a one-day event; the incumbent-pressure framing only holds if a bank or a competitor responds in the window.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, incumbent as protagonist. The story sits with the rail being displaced, not with Wise. Every other angle here keeps Wise or its customer at the centre.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual settlement time and cost versus a comparable correspondent-banking transfer, on the same corridor, with the fee stack shown; what portion of the £30B quarterly volume is business (not consumer) payments; whether any correspondent-bank relationship is being replaced or merely supplemented; a named bank or payments incumbent willing to respond.
- **Facts it rests on:** businesses can hold and settle in a regulated stablecoin to move money across borders faster and cheaper than the correspondent-banking system; Wise moves over £30B in cross-border volume per quarter.

**A profitable listed payments company is putting stablecoins on its balance-sheet flows**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A markets or financial-services reporter covering listed fintech — someone who writes for shareholders and analysts, not product users. The hook is the issuer profile: not a startup with a token thesis, but a profitable public company with disclosure obligations moving regulated stablecoin settlement into a live business line. That reporter can ask what it does to treasury, custody risk, and reporting. Not for product-review desks, crypto-native outlets, or SMB how-to press.
- **Why now:** The launch is today, and it is the first time this flow is being described as a shipped feature rather than a position.
- **Decay:** week — the listed-company treatment can run past launch day if there is an executive on record or an analyst reaction.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up to the market rung, with the power-elite news value doing the work: the significance is *who* is doing this, not what the feature does. The counterposition angle above is about the rail; this one is about the issuer's risk and disclosure profile.
- **Proof it needs:** which entity holds the stablecoin balances and under what custody arrangement; what the company has said publicly about balance-sheet and treasury exposure; whether this appears in any filing or investor communication; the named stablecoin and partner — without that name this angle cannot survive an analyst's first question.
- **Facts it rests on:** the company is publicly listed and profitable; businesses can hold and settle in a regulated stablecoin; the move is framed as extending a mission of cheap, transparent transfers.

**Stablecoin settlement arrives one regulator at a time**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A payments-policy or compliance reporter at a regulatory trade publication covering stablecoin authorisation regimes across jurisdictions. The interesting fact is the rollout shape: a regulated firm is shipping the same product at different speeds because approval varies by market, which makes the product a live map of where stablecoin payments are actually permitted. Not for consumer press, product desks, or crypto-price coverage.
- **Why now:** The region-by-region rollout is being disclosed now; the regulatory-fragmentation story it evidences runs longer than launch day.
- **Decay:** week — a policy-context piece, not an event story; it goes cold when a regulator moves and changes the map.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the regulator as protagonist. The fact used here is the one the other angles skip — that approval varies by market — and the protagonist is the rulebook, not the company, the buyer, or the incumbent.
- **Proof it needs:** the specific list of launch markets and the specific authorisation each depends on; which markets are explicitly excluded and why; a compliance or policy lead willing to speak on record about the approval process; confirmation of what "regulated stablecoin" means legally in each named market. Do not pitch this without the market list — it is the entire story.
- **Facts it rests on:** regulatory approval varies by market and the feature rolls out region by region; businesses can hold and settle in a regulated stablecoin.

**The businesses waiting three days for a payment that clears in minutes**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A trade reporter covering finance operations for internationally trading SMEs — import/export, freelancer marketplaces, or a corporate-treasury trade title. The reader is the finance lead who reconciles the settlement lag, not the payments industry. This reporter needs one company describing what the delay costs them in working capital, and what changes when settlement is same-day. Not for markets desks, policy reporters, or crypto outlets.
- **Why now:** Honestly weak on urgency. The feature launching gives a peg, but the underlying pain is not time-pressured; this angle survives on the customer, not the clock.
- **Decay:** month — a concrete customer story keeps for weeks if the customer is real and named.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed to the bottom rung — one buyer, one corridor, one number. The counterposition angle argues about a system; this one shows a business's cash position.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named business customer on record with the corridor they pay into, their previous settlement time, and the working-capital cost of the lag; whether that customer is actually live on the feature today or in a pilot; any FX or fee comparison they can substantiate. Without a named customer this angle does not exist — do not pitch it as a hypothetical.
- **Facts it rests on:** businesses can hold and settle in a regulated stablecoin to move money across borders faster and cheaper than the correspondent-banking system.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- You are not naming the stablecoin or the partner. That is the first question every one of these reporters asks, and "we can't say" ends most of these conversations at the second email. Is the name embargoed, unsigned, or deliberately withheld? The answer changes whether you pitch now or wait.
- "Faster and cheaper than correspondent banking" is the load-bearing claim in three of these angles and you have supplied no number behind it. Faster by how much, on which corridor, against what baseline fee? Without that, a reporter has your marketing comparison and nothing to check it against.
- £30B a quarter is total cross-border volume, not stablecoin volume. What share of business flows is actually eligible for this on day one — and are you prepared for the answer to be printed?
- Is there a named business customer live on this today who will talk? If not, the customer-story angle dies and the other three lose their concrete floor.
- "Regulated stablecoin" is doing a lot of work. Regulated by whom, under which regime, and in which of your launch markets? A compliance reporter will treat a vague answer as a red flag rather than a detail.
- Are you prepared for the question about what happens to customer balances if the issuer fails or a regulator suspends the instrument? Nothing in the facts addresses it, and a listed company launching this will be asked.
