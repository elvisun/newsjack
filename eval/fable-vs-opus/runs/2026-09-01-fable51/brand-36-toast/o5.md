**Toast crossed 120,000 locations and now wants to be the restaurant industry's lender**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A fintech-infrastructure reporter at a payments or embedded-finance trade outlet covering software companies that become balance-sheet businesses. The hook is not the location count — it's that a point-of-sale vendor with 120,000 locations and tens of billions in processed sales is now underwriting those same merchants. That reporter can test whether restaurant POS is becoming a credit distribution channel rather than a software market. Not for restaurant-tech product reviewers, consumer dining press, or general startup desks.
- **Why now:** The bundling of lending and payroll into the platform is the current move, and 120,000 locations is the fresh number that gives it scale.
- **Decay:** week — the milestone is a 24-hour item, but the "POS becomes a lender" category thesis holds for a reporting week.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — the protagonist is the category (embedded finance inside vertical SaaS), not Toast's product line or any single restaurant. The other angles drop to the merchant, to the margin math, and to the fee complaints.
- **Proof it needs:** the size of the Toast Capital book (originations, outstanding balances); what share of the 120,000 locations use Capital and payroll versus hardware only; whether Toast lends off its own balance sheet or through a bank partner; a named comparable POS or vertical-SaaS company doing the same thing, supplied by you, not asserted.
- **Facts it rests on:** Toast crossed 120,000 restaurant locations; it is bundling lending and payroll into the platform; Toast Capital offers cash advances repaid as a share of daily sales; it processes tens of billions in annual restaurant sales.

**Your cash register is also your lender: what daily-sales repayment does to a restaurant's cash flow**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A restaurant-operations reporter at a hospitality or small-business trade outlet covering unit economics and working capital for independent operators. The mechanic is the story: an advance repaid as a share of daily sales comes out automatically through the same system that takes the payments, so a slow month repays slower and a strong month repays faster — and the operator never writes a check. That reporter's readers are the ones signing these agreements. Not for fintech VC newsletters, national business desks, or payments-technology press that covers this from the vendor's side.
- **Why now:** Honestly weak as a news hook on its own. The reason to run it now is that Toast is actively bundling the product across a 120,000-location base, so the number of operators exposed to the mechanic is growing.
- **Decay:** month — an operator-economics explainer with no event clock behind it.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, customer protagonist, at the bottom rung of the ladder. Same fact as the category angle, but the reader is a restaurant owner deciding whether to take an advance, not an investor sizing a market.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named restaurant operator who took an advance and will describe the terms, the holdback percentage, and what it cost in effective annualized terms; the actual repayment share and any fixed fee; what happens on a bad month or a closure; whether Toast can adjust the holdback mid-term.
- **Facts it rests on:** Toast Capital offers cash advances repaid as a share of daily sales; the platform serves 120,000 restaurant locations; some restaurant owners have complained about Toast's fees.

**Toast reached profitability by selling loans, not hardware**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A markets or equity-research reporter at a financial news outlet covering newly profitable public software companies and the quality of their earnings. The story is the mix shift: Toast says fintech and lending carry higher margins than hardware, and Toast recently reached profitability — so the open question is how much of that profit is credit income, and how durable credit income is across a restaurant cycle. Not for restaurant trade press, product reviewers, or founder-profile outlets.
- **Why now:** Toast is publicly traded and recently reached profitability, and the lending push is being expanded now — the two facts belong in the same paragraph.
- **Decay:** week — a margin-mix story runs on the current disclosure, but it goes cold when the next quarterly report resets the numbers.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens (relationships — what profitability correlates with), with the investor/markets reader as protagonist. The category angle argues a market thesis; this one interrogates a P&L.
- **Proof it needs:** segment-level revenue and gross margin for fintech versus hardware versus subscription, from filings; the dollar contribution of Toast Capital to profit; take rate on processed volume; whether "recently reached profitability" is GAAP net income, operating income, or adjusted — say which.
- **Facts it rests on:** Toast is publicly traded and recently reached profitability; it says lending and fintech are higher-margin than its hardware business; it processes tens of billions in annual restaurant sales; Toast Capital offers cash advances.

**Toast lends to restaurants and won't say how many fail to repay**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-finance or small-business-credit reporter at an outlet that covers merchant cash advances and non-bank lending, including policy-adjacent desks tracking small-business credit disclosure. The prevailing belief is that embedded lending is a low-risk, data-advantaged business because the lender sits on the merchant's payment flow and sees revenue in real time. Undisclosed default rates on advances to restaurants — an industry with well-known failure rates — is the evidence that cuts against it. Not for anyone covering this as a product launch, and not for restaurant trade press that would treat it as a vendor story.
- **Why now:** Toast is expanding lending across a growing merchant base while default rates remain undisclosed; the expansion is what makes the missing number newsworthy rather than academic.
- **Decay:** week — the gap stays open until Toast discloses or an analyst forces the question.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — the angle is built on an absence, not a claim. Where the data angle asks how profitable lending is, this one asks what the profit figure could be hiding. Different protagonist (the borrower and the disclosure regime) and a different beat.
- **Proof it needs:** the named prevailing belief in a citable form — analyst notes or filings arguing embedded lending is lower-risk, which you must supply; confirmation from filings or investor materials that default, loss, or charge-off rates on Capital are genuinely not broken out; any reserve or credit-loss line item that could serve as a proxy; a credit analyst willing to say on record what the omission means. Do not pitch this without the filings check — the whole angle collapses if the number is disclosed somewhere.
- **Facts it rests on:** Toast Capital offers cash advances repaid as a share of daily sales; default rates on advances are not disclosed; Toast is publicly traded; the company is bundling lending into the platform.

**The more Toast a restaurant buys, the harder it is to leave**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A competition or vertical-software reporter at a business or technology outlet covering platform lock-in and buyer power in concentrated supplier markets. Once payments, payroll and credit run through one vendor, switching costs stop being a software migration and become a payroll cutover plus an outstanding loan — and existing fee complaints are the audible early signal. That reporter can look at what alternatives independent operators actually have. Not for fintech product press or anyone who would frame it as a feature-comparison piece.
- **Why now:** The bundling is happening now, alongside a 120,000-location footprint — the scale is what makes lock-in a market question rather than a customer-service one.
- **Decay:** month — a structural power story with no single event driving it.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, competitor/market-structure protagonist, using the conflict news value. The customer-story angle is about one operator's cash flow; this is about what the bundle does to the whole buyer side of the market.
- **Proof it needs:** documented fee complaints with sources — forum posts, trade-press coverage, or operators willing to go on record, since "some owners have complained" is not pitchable as-is; Toast's contract terms on termination while an advance is outstanding; the real cost and duration of switching POS and payroll; a competitor or an independent-restaurant association willing to comment. You must supply the complaint evidence; do not characterize the complaints beyond what you can show.
- **Facts it rests on:** Toast is bundling lending and payroll into the platform; some restaurant owners have complained about Toast's fees; 120,000 restaurant locations; payroll targets restaurants juggling tipped and hourly staff.

**Refused angles**

- "Toast transforms the future of restaurant technology with 120,000 locations" — `slop`. Press-release headline with no story under it.
- "Toast's payroll product solves the tipped-wage headache for restaurant operators" — `no_journalist_shape`. The only fact is that the product targets restaurants with tipped and hourly staff. There is no tip-credit rule, no regulatory change, no compliance failure, and no customer here — that is a product page, not a story. Bring a specific tipped-wage rule this product responds to and it becomes a real defensive-comment or trend angle.
- "Toast's lending push is a bet against the small-business lending crunch" — `hallucinated_fact`. No credit-market condition was supplied. Inventing a lending environment to peg against is exactly the forced relevance that a credit reporter catches on one read.
- "What 120,000 locations means for the restaurant industry" — `duplicate`. Collapses into the category-creation angle with a vaguer beat; the milestone is scale evidence, not a separate story.
- "Toast proves vertical SaaS companies must become fintechs to survive" — `hallucinated_fact`. One company reaching profitability with a higher-margin fintech line is not evidence about an industry-wide requirement. This would need comparable segment data across several vertical-SaaS companies.
- "Toast's profitability shows disciplined execution in a tough market" — `slop`. Undefended market characterization wrapped around a fact that already has a sharper home in the data angle.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Whose side are you on here? The lock-in and undisclosed-default angles are adversarial toward Toast. If you are pitching on Toast's behalf, angles four and five are not yours to pitch — they are the stories a reporter will write anyway once you draw attention to the lending bundle, and you should be prepared to answer them rather than place them. Say which seat you are in before anything goes out.
- Are the default rates genuinely undisclosed, or just not in the material you were given? A public company's filings may break out credit losses somewhere. Pitching "they won't say" when they said it in a 10-Q ends the relationship with that reporter.
- Can you produce a single named restaurant operator who will talk about a Toast Capital advance on record, with terms? Without one, the strongest operator-economics angle is a mechanics explainer with no human in it, and it will lose to a piece that has one.
- What does "some restaurant owners have complained about fees" actually consist of — a handful of forum posts, a trade-press story, a lawsuit, a regulator inquiry? The counterposition angle is either well-evidenced or it is gossip, and the answer changes whether it is pitchable at all.
- Is "recently reached profitability" GAAP, operating, or adjusted, and over what period? A markets reporter will ask in the first reply, and getting it wrong in a pitch is disqualifying.
- Do you have any current news signal here? Everything above is built on the company's own facts. There is no breaking hook in this material, which is why nothing carries a decay tag shorter than a week. A live peg — an earnings date, a regulatory move on merchant cash advances, a competitor's lending launch — would materially strengthen the first, third, and fourth angles.
