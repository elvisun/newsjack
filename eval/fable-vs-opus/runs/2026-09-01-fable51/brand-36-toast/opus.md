**Toast crosses 120,000 restaurant locations as it pivots from hardware to fintech margins**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A fintech or payments reporter at a business/markets trade outlet covering how vertical software companies become lenders. The hook is not the location count — it's the business-model shift: a point-of-sale hardware company now leaning on lending and payroll because they're higher-margin than the terminals it's known for. This reporter can test whether Toast is best understood as a restaurant-tech company or as a fintech wearing restaurant clothing. Not for general consumer tech press, local restaurant-opening reporters, or gadget desks.
- **Why now:** The 120,000-location milestone plus the recent move to profitability give a markets reporter a live reason to re-examine what Toast actually is now. The model shift itself is a longer-running story.
- **Decay:** week — the milestone is a 24-hour peg, but the "POS company becomes a fintech" thesis supports a week-long analysis piece.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — this makes the *business model* the protagonist, not the milestone or any single restaurant. The other angles drop down to the borrowing restaurant and over to the public-market investor.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual revenue or margin split between hardware, payments, lending, and payroll; how much of recent profitability is attributable to fintech vs. hardware; any company statement on file about the margin claim beyond a general assertion.
- **Facts it rests on:** Toast crossed 120,000 restaurant locations; it is bundling lending and payroll into the platform; it says lending and fintech are higher-margin than its hardware business; it recently reached profitability and is publicly traded.

**A cash advance repaid from your daily sales: how Toast Capital actually works for a restaurant**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A restaurant-trade or small-business reporter who covers how operators finance themselves — the buyer's-world beat. The entry point is the repayment mechanic: an advance repaid as a share of daily sales is structurally different from a bank loan, and an operator can explain what that feels like in a slow month. Not for fintech-thesis reporters (that's the other angle) or general business-news desks that won't get into restaurant cash flow.
- **Why now:** Honestly thin on urgency. The product exists now and the milestone makes Toast topical, but there is no event forcing this story today — it's close to evergreen and should be pitched as an operator-finance feature, not breaking news.
- **Decay:** month — operator-finance features have a long shelf life; no event pins them to a date.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, customer protagonist — the borrowing restaurant is the story, not Toast's model or its stock. Different beat (restaurant operations) and different altitude (one operator's books) from the category angle.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named restaurant owner who has used Toast Capital and will describe the terms and experience on record; the actual cost of an advance (fees, effective rate, repayment share); what happens during a sales slump. Without a real, named operator this is a topic, not a story.
- **Facts it rests on:** Toast Capital offers cash advances repaid as a share of daily sales; Toast processes tens of billions in annual restaurant sales.

**The number Toast won't give you: default rates on its restaurant cash advances**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A fintech-accountability or financial-investigations reporter at a business outlet that scrutinizes lenders. The prevailing belief is that a newly profitable, publicly traded platform lending against its own customers' sales has a clean, well-understood book. The undermining evidence: default rates on the advances are not disclosed, and some restaurant owners are already complaining about fees. The story is the gap between "profitable fintech" and "won't show the loss rate." Not for product reviewers, founder-profile writers, or anyone who'd take the margin claim at face value.
- **Why now:** Recent profitability and the deepening push into lending make non-disclosure of default rates a fair, timely question now — investors and operators both have a stake.
- **Decay:** week — tied to the profitability and expansion news, but the disclosure question stays live for a reporting cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — it names a belief (a profitable public lender is transparent and low-risk) and points at evidence against it (undisclosed defaults plus fee complaints). Opposite framing from the category and customer angles, which take the model and product as given.
- **Proof it needs:** confirmation the default rate is genuinely undisclosed in public filings (a reporter must verify, not assume); the specific nature and number of the fee complaints, not a vague "some owners"; whether the merchant-cash-advance structure is regulated as lending in relevant jurisdictions. The contrarian frame collapses if defaults are in fact disclosed somewhere.
- **Facts it rests on:** default rates on advances are not disclosed; some restaurant owners have complained about Toast's fees; Toast recently reached profitability and is publicly traded; lending is part of its higher-margin push.
