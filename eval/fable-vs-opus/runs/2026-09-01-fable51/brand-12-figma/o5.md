**Figma ships a design-to-code tool while the Adobe deal is dead and the IPO clock is unstated**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A design-tools trade reporter or product-strategy reporter at a design/creative-software publication covering the Figma-vs-Adobe power balance. The story isn't the feature; it's what an independent Figma builds now that it isn't being absorbed. This beat has been following the Adobe acquisition thread and needs the next chapter. Not for general consumer tech desks, not for AI-model reporters, not for enterprise IT desks.
- **Why now:** The launch is the first substantive product move framed against the collapsed Adobe acquisition — that's the peg, and it's live today.
- **Decay:** 24hr — product launches are a one-day event; the Adobe framing extends it only if Figma will speak on record about strategy.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, incumbent/competitor protagonist. The story is the category's power balance, not the tool's capabilities or the buyer's experience.
- **Proof it needs:** an on-record executive willing to connect the product roadmap to the post-Adobe strategy; a clear statement of what Make does that the incumbent's design-to-code tooling doesn't; confirmation of what Figma will and won't say about IPO timing before a reporter asks (they will ask).
- **Facts it rests on:** Figma is launching Figma Make, which exports React and HTML/CSS from a Figma file; the launch follows the collapse of its acquisition by Adobe; no IPO timing disclosed.

**Figma puts a $25 price on the handoff between designers and front-end engineers**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A SaaS-business or software-pricing reporter at a B2B business outlet who covers how vendors monetize AI features. The hook is the packaging decision: a separate $25/user/month Make seat stacked on existing plans, rather than folding AI into the base tier. That's a testable bet about who pays — and whether engineering budget or design budget absorbs it. Not for product-review outlets, not for design-craft press, not for AI research reporters.
- **Why now:** The pricing is disclosed today with the launch; the "does AI get its own SKU" question is being decided across the software category right now, and this is a concrete data point in it.
- **Decay:** week — pricing-strategy analysis survives past launch day if the numbers hold up.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the money as protagonist — the seat model is the story, not the product or the users. Distinct from the Adobe angle, which is about competitive position, not monetization.
- **Proof it needs:** whether the Make seat is required per designer, per engineer, or both; whether existing plan pricing changed; any early signal on attach rate or which budget line customers pay from; whether the AI design assistant is included in the base plan or bundled into the Make seat. Without at least the seat-scope answer, this angle is a press-release rewrite.
- **Facts it rests on:** pricing adds a new 'Make' seat at $25/user/month on top of existing plans; Make exports React and HTML/CSS from a Figma file; the AI assistant generates layouts from a text prompt.

**What happens to front-end work when the design file compiles**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A developer-tooling or engineering-practice reporter at a technical publication or developer-focused newsletter covering how AI codegen changes the shape of front-end jobs. Design-to-code has been promised for a decade and has repeatedly produced unmaintainable output; a vendor with 4 million users shipping it is the reason to revisit the question now. This reporter will want to run the exported code, not read a spec sheet. Not for design-leadership press, not for business desks, not for HR/future-of-work columnists.
- **Why now:** The launch supplies the peg; the underlying question about AI-generated front-end code is an active running story that this fits into.
- **Decay:** week — trend framing outlives the launch day, but only while the launch is still the freshest example.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — the fact becomes evidence about front-end practice rather than a product story. Different altitude and different protagonist from the pricing and Adobe angles.
- **Proof it needs:** a real exported React component a reporter can inspect and run; what the output does with state, accessibility, and existing component libraries; whether teams ship the export or treat it as a starting draft; an engineering lead at a customer willing to describe what they actually merged. This angle dies without inspectable code — Figma's claim about what Make produces is not sufficient.
- **Facts it rests on:** Make exports React and HTML/CSS from a Figma file; the AI assistant generates layouts from a text prompt; Figma says it has over 4 million users and is used at most Fortune 500 companies.

**The Fortune 500 design tool is now asking to write production code**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** An enterprise software reporter at a CIO/IT trade outlet covering procurement, code provenance, and security review for AI-generated code. If a tool already inside most Fortune 500 companies starts emitting code that ships to production, it walks into a different approval process than a design tool ever faced. That's a concrete procurement story, not a feature story. Not for design press, not for startup/product blogs, not for consumer tech.
- **Why now:** The capability is announced today; enterprise buyers have to form a position on AI-generated code entering their repos.
- **Decay:** week — the procurement question is not a same-day story, but it's tied to the launch being current.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the enterprise buyer as protagonist, zoomed down to the deployment reality. The developer-practice angle asks whether the code is good; this asks whether a security and compliance team will let it in.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named enterprise customer willing to describe their review process for Make output — without a named customer this collapses into speculation; what Figma can say about licensing, provenance, and indemnity for generated code; whether "used at most Fortune 500 companies" means paid seats or any usage, with a source for the claim; whether the Make seat is available under existing enterprise agreements.
- **Facts it rests on:** Figma says it has over 4 million users and is used at most Fortune 500 companies; Make exports React and HTML/CSS from a Figma file; pricing adds a new 'Make' seat at $25/user/month.

**Refused angles**

- "Figma unveils revolutionary AI that transforms the future of design" — `slop`. Press-release headline, undefended superlative, no story.
- "Figma Make signals designers will replace engineers" — `hallucinated_fact`. Nothing in the update supports a claim about job displacement; no data, no customer, no evidence.
- "Figma's launch shows it never needed Adobe" — `hallucinated_fact`. The update says the acquisition collapsed. It says nothing about Figma's independent performance, revenue, or whether the product validates the outcome.
- "Figma preps for an IPO with a new revenue line" — `hallucinated_fact`. No IPO timing is disclosed. Writing the IPO as the frame invents the central claim, and a reporter will ask for the confirmation you don't have.
- "AI design assistant generates layouts from a prompt" — `duplicate`. Same product, same protagonist as the codegen trend angle; there's no separate beat that cares about the prompt-to-layout feature on its own from these facts.
- "What Figma Make means for designers' careers" — `no_journalist_shape`. No sub-beat, no evidence, no protagonist. This is a topic.
- "Figma's $25 seat and the state of the design-tool market" — `duplicate`. Collapses into the pricing angle with a vaguer beat and weaker proof.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Can you produce one named customer — enterprise or otherwise — who has used Make and will talk? Two of the four angles above are thin without it, and the enterprise angle is dead without it.
- Can a reporter get hands-on access to run the exported code before writing? If not, the developer-tooling angle turns into a rewrite of your announcement, and that beat will notice.
- What is Figma's actual position on IPO timing? Every reporter you pitch will ask, whatever the angle is. Decide the answer before the first call, not during it.
- Does "used at most Fortune 500 companies" mean paid enterprise contracts or any account with a corporate email? Reporters check this kind of claim, and the weaker reading damages the stronger claim.
- Is there a competitor response already in the market for design-to-code? I have no current signal on that, so the counterposition angle rests on the Adobe history alone — which is real, but a year of distance may have cooled it. Worth checking before you pitch it as the lead.
- Was there an announcement embargo, and has it lifted? Nothing in the facts says either way, and it changes what can go out today.
