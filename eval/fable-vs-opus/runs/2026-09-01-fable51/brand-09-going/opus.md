**Going turns a mistake-fare alert into a trip you can actually take**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-travel-tech reporter at a mainstream travel trade or a consumer-tech desk that covers how travel-booking tools evolve. The hook isn't "another AI feature" — it's the shift from deal-alert service to planning layer: the deal that lands in your inbox now comes with a day-by-day itinerary, hotel options, and a packing list attached. The interesting tension is that the planning still ends at a link-out, not a booking. A reporter can probe whether "alert-to-itinerary" is a real product category or a feature bolted onto a deals business. Not for enterprise-AI press, general LLM-tooling coverage, or hard-news desks.
- **Why now:** The planner launches now; that's the honest peg. The "is alert-to-planning a category" question is a slower-burning trend that survives past launch day.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the launch itself is standard product news; the category framing can stretch to a week if the reporter wants the trend treatment.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — this makes the product *category* (deal-discovery glued to planning) the story, not the company or the user. The other angles drop to the traveler and cut against the AI-planner hype.
- **Proof it needs:** a working demo showing a real fare alert producing an itinerary; clarity on what the planner generates versus what a user still does by hand; the share of the ~2M member base that can actually use it (Premium-only); any data on itineraries generated or used since launch.
- **Facts it rests on:** Going launched an AI trip planner that turns a cheap-fare alert into a day-by-day itinerary, hotel options, and a packing list; the planner is Premium-only; no in-app booking yet, only planning and links out.

**The AI travel planner that books nothing**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A travel-tech or consumer-tech reporter covering the wave of "AI will plan and book your whole trip" announcements. The prevailing belief, loudly marketed across the industry, is that AI agents will soon handle the entire trip end to end including the transaction. Going's launch cuts against that: it deliberately plans and links out, with no airline or hotel booking happening in-app. That's a genuine surprise worth a reported piece on why a company would stop short of booking — friction, liability, margins, or airline relationships. Not for outlets that just want a feature roundup.
- **Why now:** The launch is the peg, and it lands while "AI agents that book everything" is the dominant industry narrative — the contrast is timely.
- **Decay:** week — the contrarian read on the broader "AI books your trip" narrative outlives the launch-day cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — it names the widely-held "AI will book the whole trip" belief and uses Going's no-booking design as evidence against the inevitability of that. Distinct from the category angle, which takes the launch at face value rather than questioning the dominant narrative.
- **Proof it needs:** an on-record reason from Going for stopping at planning rather than booking (the prevailing belief is documented industry-wide, but the company's rationale is not in the facts); whether booking is on the roadmap; how the link-out experience actually works.
- **Facts it rests on:** the planner only does planning and links out; no airline or hotel booking happens in-app yet; the feature drafts itineraries, hotel options, and a packing list.

**Going's new planner is a Premium-only perk — and most of its 2 million members can't touch it**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer or personal-finance reporter who covers subscription value, freemium gating, and whether "upgrade to unlock" perks are worth the money. The frame: Going has ~2 million free members but the headline AI feature is locked to its smaller paid Premium/Elite base. That's a clean consumer-value question — what does the planner add on top of the deal alerts free members already get, and is it enough to justify the upgrade. Not for pure travel-deal aggregators or enterprise-software desks.
- **Why now:** The Premium-only gating ships with the launch; the upgrade-value question is live the moment free members see a feature they can't use.
- **Decay:** 24hr — tied to the launch news cycle; softens after.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the customer/buyer as protagonist, framed as free-vs-paid tension. Different from the category angle (market-level) and the contrarian angle (the no-booking design) — here the story is the paywall and whether it earns the upgrade.
- **Proof it needs:** Premium/Elite pricing and the size of the paid base; what free members get versus paid; whether the cited "$550 average savings per booked flight" applies to paid members specifically and how it's measured; any data on upgrade conversion since launch.
- **Facts it rests on:** ~2 million free members and a smaller paid Premium/Elite base; the planner is Premium-only; the company says paid members save an average of $550 per booked flight.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- How much of the planner is genuinely AI-generated versus templated? "Day-by-day itinerary, hotel options, packing list" is a thin spec — a reporter will ask what the model actually does that a checklist couldn't.
- Can you put a real fare alert through the planner in a live demo and show the output? Without that, the strongest launch angles stay claims, not stories.
- The "$550 average savings per booked flight" — how is it measured, over what period, against what baseline, and does it apply to all paid members or a subset? This number is doing heavy lifting and a deals-skeptical reporter will press it.
- If the planner only links out and books nothing, what stops a free member from getting the same itinerary idea elsewhere? The Premium-only value story depends on the answer.
- How many Premium/Elite members are there, and how many have used the planner since launch? "Smaller paid base" with no number is a hole a reporter will see.
