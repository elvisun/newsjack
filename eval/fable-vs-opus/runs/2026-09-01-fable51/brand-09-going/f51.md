**Going's new AI planner lets the airfare pick the destination**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A travel-tech reporter at a travel trade outlet, or on the travel desk of a consumer tech outlet, covering how people decide where to go. Most trip-planning tools start from a destination; this one starts from a price the app found and works backward to an itinerary, hotel options, and a packing list. That inversion is the story, not the AI label. Not for enterprise-AI desks, VC or markets reporters, or airline-operations trade press.
- **Why now:** The planner is launching now. Without a supplied news signal, the launch itself is the only hook.
- **Decay:** week. Launch news is a 24-hour event, but the "price-first travel" framing carries a short trend window.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up. The traveler's decision process is the protagonist. The other kept angles are about the business model, the distribution choice, and a number.
- **Proof it needs:** A live demo or screenshots of one real alert turning into one real itinerary; a plain answer on whether hotel options and packing lists are pulled from live data or generated by the model; at least one usage figure from beta or launch week.
- **Facts it rests on:** The planner triggers when the app finds a mistake fare or deep discount; it drafts a day-by-day itinerary, hotel options, and a packing list; the planner is launching now.

**Going puts its AI planner behind the paywall to convert 2 million free deal-hunters**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A subscription-economy or consumer-business reporter covering how newsletter-born audiences get monetized as software. Going grew a roughly 2-million-member free list as Scott's Cheap Flights and now gates its first AI feature to Premium. The question this reporter will test is whether AI features are becoming the standard upsell lever for consumer subscriptions. Not for lifestyle travel writers, product-review columns, or general AI desks.
- **Why now:** The Premium-only decision is being made public with the launch.
- **Decay:** week. The conversion story holds as long as the launch is fresh; it dies if the company won't share paid-base numbers.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, company-as-business protagonist. This is about who pays and why, not what the product does or how travelers choose.
- **Proof it needs:** The size of the Premium and Elite base, or at minimum a conversion rate before and after launch; the Premium price; an on-record reason the planner is paid-only rather than a free hook.
- **Facts it rests on:** Going was formerly Scott's Cheap Flights; roughly 2 million free members; a smaller paid Premium/Elite base; the planner is Premium-only.

**Going built an AI trip planner that stops short of booking**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A travel-distribution trade reporter covering the power balance between airlines, online travel agencies, and the tools that send them customers. Going plans the trip and then links out; it does not sell the ticket or the room. That puts it on one side of a live industry question about who owns the transaction. Not for consumer lifestyle travel press or general AI-hype coverage.
- **Why now:** The launch makes the no-booking choice visible. The word "yet" in the company's own description means this window may close on the company's own roadmap.
- **Decay:** week. This is a positioning story with a launch peg, and it decays fast if booking is announced next.
- **What makes it distinct:** Conflict lens. The protagonist is the industry's booking layer and Going's decision not to enter it. Neither the traveler angle nor the paywall angle touches distribution.
- **Proof it needs:** An on-record answer on why booking is not in the app; whether the outbound links earn affiliate or referral revenue; whether any airline or hotel is a formal partner; whether "yet" means booking is on a dated roadmap.
- **Facts it rests on:** No airline or hotel booking happens in-app; the planner offers planning and links out; the company's own phrasing says "yet."

**The math behind Going's claim that paid members save $550 a flight**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A personal-finance reporter covering whether travel subscriptions pay for themselves. The number is the company's, so the reporter's story is how it is measured, against what baseline, and whether adding a planner changes the value case. Not for AI-product reviewers, travel trade press, or startup-funding desks.
- **Why now:** The paid tier just gained a feature, so the "is Premium worth it" question is being re-asked. This angle is otherwise not time-pressured.
- **Decay:** week. The launch gives it a peg; without the methodology it has no story at all.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, scale value. The protagonist is a single number and its provenance. No other kept angle depends on the savings claim.
- **Proof it needs:** The methodology: sample size, time period, what "average" means, and what fare the savings are measured against; the Premium price so a reader can do the payback math; willingness to have a reporter check the calculation.
- **Facts it rests on:** The company says paid members save an average of $550 per booked flight; the planner is Premium-only.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- How big is the paid base? "Smaller" is not a number. The paywall angle needs it or a conversion rate, or it collapses.
- How is $550 calculated, against what baseline, over what period? If the answer is a marketing estimate, cut that angle rather than pitch it.
- What happens to an itinerary built on a mistake fare the airline later cancels? A travel reporter will ask this in the first email.
- Are hotel options and packing lists grounded in live availability and destination data, or generated by the model? A reporter will test it on a real fare.
- Do the outbound links earn referral revenue? If yes, the "we don't book" story is an affiliate story, which is a different pitch.
- Is booking on a dated roadmap? If it lands within the quarter, the counterposition angle should not be pitched at all.
- Has anyone outside the company used the planner? One real launch-week usage figure changes every angle above.
