**Going's AI trip planner turns a $200 mistake fare into a booked week — for paying members only**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-travel reporter at a personal-finance or travel-service desk who covers the deal-alert economy and what subscription travel tools actually deliver. The hook is the paywall placement: the free tier finds the fare, the paid tier tells you what to do with it. That reporter can test whether the planner is worth the subscription against free alternatives their readers already use. Not for enterprise AI press, airline-industry trade reporters, or startup-funding desks — there is no round and no B2B story here.
- **Why now:** The planner is launching now, which is the only time hook. Nothing else in the facts is time-pressured.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard launch news; the tiering question can carry a service piece for longer if a reporter tests the product.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, competitor/incumbent protagonist — this angle is about where Going sits against free AI planning tools and the rest of the deal-alert market, not about the member base or the savings number.
- **Proof it needs:** what the planner does that a free general-purpose chatbot handed the same fare does not; the Premium and Elite price points; whether Elite gets anything the Premium tier doesn't; a live demo or press access so a reporter can run their own fare through it.
- **Facts it rests on:** the planner drafts a day-by-day itinerary, hotel options, and a packing list off a mistake fare or deep discount; the planner is Premium-only; Going has ~2 million free members and a smaller paid base.

**Two million people get the cheap flight. A fraction of them get the trip.**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A subscription-economy or consumer-business reporter at a business outlet covering how freemium companies convert large free audiences — the beat that writes about conversion rates, paywall design, and where consumer subscriptions are heading. The story is the business decision: Going has a 2-million-person free funnel and is putting its most obviously AI-shaped feature behind the paid wall as a conversion lever. Not for travel-service desks writing "best flight deal apps" roundups, and not for AI-model press.
- **Why now:** The paywall decision is only visible because the feature is shipping today; without the launch there's no reason to examine the tier design now.
- **Decay:** week — the business-model question outlives the launch cycle if an executive will discuss it on record.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the money as protagonist — the reader is a business reporter asking about unit economics, not a traveler asking whether to subscribe. Different altitude and different beat from the consumer angle above.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual free-to-paid conversion rate, or at minimum the paid-member count Going is willing to publish; an on-record exec explaining why the planner is Premium-only rather than a free hook; what the planner costs Going to run per itinerary; whether conversion has moved since launch.
- **Facts it rests on:** ~2 million free members and a smaller paid Premium/Elite base; the planner is Premium-only.

**The $550 claim: what a flight-deal subscription actually saves, and how it's counted**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-finance or data-driven service reporter at a money outlet who pressure-tests advertised savings claims — the beat that asks "compared to what?" for cashback apps, insurance comparison sites, and subscription services. The $550-per-booked-flight average is the company's central number and it is a reportable claim: what's the baseline fare, is it a mean or a median, does it count only members who booked. Not for travel-lifestyle writers or destination desks, who won't interrogate the methodology.
- **Why now:** The launch is the occasion to put the number in front of a reporter, but the claim itself is not time-sensitive. Honest read: this angle is peg-light and rests on Going's willingness to open the methodology.
- **Decay:** week — a data/service piece, not an event story.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, scale — the number is the story, not the product or the business model. It requires a methodology disclosure the other angles don't need, and it can run without mentioning the planner at all.
- **Proof it needs:** the methodology behind $550 — sample size, time period, baseline price source, mean versus median, whether it counts only bookings made through Going alerts; ideally the underlying dataset or a third party who has checked it. Without this, do not pitch this angle at all; an unopened savings claim is what gets a story killed at the fact-check stage.
- **Facts it rests on:** the company says paid members save an average of $550 per booked flight; the paid base is smaller than the ~2 million free members.

**The AI trip planner that won't sell you the trip**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A travel-industry reporter at a trade outlet covering distribution and booking channels — the beat that follows who owns the transaction between traveler and airline. The interesting fact is the absence: Going plans the trip in full and then links out rather than booking. That reporter can ask whether a planning layer that doesn't book is a stepping stone to becoming an OTA, a deliberate choice to avoid airline distribution deals, or simply a product that isn't finished. Not for consumer deal-roundup writers, who won't care where the booking happens.
- **Why now:** The planner ships today with the booking gap in it, which makes it a fair question to ask now rather than speculatively.
- **Decay:** week — a distribution-structure story runs on the trade's own schedule, not the launch news cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — the prevailing assumption is that AI travel tools are racing to own booking; this product deliberately stops short. The protagonist is the industry structure, not Going's customers or its P&L.
- **Proof it needs:** an on-record statement about whether in-app booking is planned and on what timeline; whether Going earns affiliate revenue on the outbound links; any airline or hotel relationships that exist or are deliberately avoided. The contrarian read only holds if the choice is strategic — if booking is simply on the roadmap, this angle collapses into a product-preview story and should be dropped.
- **Facts it rests on:** no airline or hotel booking happens in-app yet, only planning and links out; the planner drafts itineraries, hotel options, and packing lists.

- "The packing-list feature travelers didn't know they needed" — `slop`. Marketing copy for a sub-feature, with no beat and no reader stakes.
- "Going's 2 million members show demand for cheap travel" — `duplicate`. Same protagonist and same underlying fact as the funnel/conversion angle, with a weaker journalist shape and no proof requirement of its own.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Can you open the $550 methodology to a reporter? If not, cut every mention of it from every pitch. A savings claim a company won't explain is the fastest way to lose a story after the reporter is already interested.
- How many paid members do you actually have? "A smaller paid base" is not a number, and the conversion angle is the strongest business story here — it dies without one.
- What does the planner do that a traveler couldn't get free from a general-purpose chatbot in five minutes? This is the first question any consumer-tech reporter asks. If you don't have a crisp answer, the launch reads as a feature, not news, and you should hold the pitch.
- Is in-app booking on the roadmap? Your answer determines whether the distribution angle is a real strategic story or a product-preview piece that dies on arrival.
- Do you earn affiliate revenue on the outbound links? A reporter will find this out; it's much better coming from you, and it materially changes how the "we don't book" framing reads.
- Is anyone available on record? Four of these angles need a named executive with a real argument, not a statement quote. Without a spokesperson, you have a product-page rewrite.
- No current news signal was supplied with this update, so nothing here is anchored to a live story. Every angle is launch-timed only.
