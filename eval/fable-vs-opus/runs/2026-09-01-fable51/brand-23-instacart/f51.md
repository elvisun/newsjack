**Instacart's delivery growth has slowed to single digits. Its ad business just passed $1 billion.**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A markets or earnings reporter at a financial outlet who covers public consumer-internet companies and revenue-mix shifts. The prevailing read on Instacart is "grocery delivery company." The facts cut against it: the delivery engine is growing in single digits while ads hit a $1B run rate and the company is profitable. This reporter can ask whether investors are pricing an ads company or a delivery company. Not for grocery trade press, ad trade press, or consumer tech reviewers.
- **Why now:** The $1B run-rate figure and the single-digit order growth are on the table at the same time. The contrast is the story, and it is fresh only while the announcement is.
- **Decay:** week. The milestone itself is a 24-hour item, but the revenue-mix question carries for a week if the company will put numbers behind it.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens, with the money as protagonist. The other kept angles look at advertisers, retailers, and store workers. This one looks at what the company is becoming and whether the market has noticed.
- **Proof it needs:** the exact quarterly order-growth figure and the period it covers; how "annual run rate" is calculated for the ad business; ads as a share of total revenue; an on-record executive willing to say where growth comes from next.
- **Facts it rests on:** ad business at a $1B annual run rate; grocery-delivery order growth slowed to single digits; Instacart is publicly traded and profitable.

**More than 6,000 brands now buy grocery ads from a company that does not own a store**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail-media reporter at an advertising or marketing trade outlet covering where consumer-brand ad budgets are moving. The count of advertisers is the hook: 6,000 brands is a breadth figure that a retail-media desk can compare to networks run by retailers themselves. Not for markets reporters, grocery-operations reporters, or general startup press.
- **Why now:** The $1B run rate and the 6,000-brand figure are new disclosures. A retail-media desk writes on the milestone day or not at all.
- **Decay:** 24hr. Standard company-metric news cycle for a trade desk.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, advertiser as protagonist, plus the magnitude value. The first angle is about Instacart's revenue mix. This one is about the brands paying for it and what they get for the money.
- **Proof it needs:** a definition of "brand" in the 6,000 figure (parent companies or individual labels); ad formats and where they appear; year-over-year advertiser and ad-revenue growth; one named brand willing to describe results. Any comparison to other retail media networks needs a source the user supplies, not a guess.
- **Facts it rests on:** ads come from over 6,000 consumer brands; ad business at a $1B annual run rate; the company partners with more than 1,500 retail banners.

**Instacart's next expansion is in the aisle, not at the door**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A store-technology or grocery-operations reporter at a grocery retail trade outlet. The retailer is the protagonist: a delivery intermediary is now putting hardware inside physical stores, with a few hundred locations live and more planned. This reporter can test how grocers feel about a partner that touches both the delivery order and the in-store trip. Not for ad trade press, markets reporters, or consumer gadget coverage.
- **Why now:** The rollout to additional chains is the news. Without named chains it is a soft peg, so the window is short.
- **Decay:** 24hr. A rollout announcement without named partners goes stale fast. With a named chain and a store count, it stretches to a week.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed down toward the store floor, with the retailer as protagonist. The first two angles are about revenue and advertisers. This one is about what a grocer is agreeing to when a Caper cart rolls onto the floor.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named grocery chain and its store count; who pays for the carts and on what terms; how items are tracked and what happens to shrink; the actual number behind "a few hundred stores" and the expansion target.
- **Facts it rests on:** Caper smart carts track items as shoppers add them and skip the checkout line; smart carts are live in a few hundred stores with plans to expand; the company partners with more than 1,500 retail banners.

**The checkout line is disappearing in a few hundred grocery stores. What happens to the people who staffed it?**

- **Story type:** defensive-comment
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail-labor or workplace-automation reporter at a business or labor-focused outlet. This reporter will read "skip the checkout line" and ask about cashier hours before asking anything about ads. Instacart should have an answer ready, and if the company has real staffing data from a live store, it can lead rather than react. Not for ad trade press, markets reporters, or grocery-tech enthusiasts.
- **Why now:** The expansion announcement invites the question now. It is not a story the company controls.
- **Decay:** week. Automation-and-jobs pieces run on their own clock once the hook lands.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, bad news alongside good news, with store workers as protagonist. Every other angle treats the cart as a growth story. This one treats it as a labor story, which is how a different beat will read it whether or not the company pitches it.
- **Proof it needs:** any staffing data from a store with carts live, on record; whether checkout staff were redeployed or reduced; a retailer willing to speak to it. Without this proof, this is a question to prepare for, not an angle to pitch.
- **Facts it rests on:** Caper smart carts skip the checkout line; smart carts are live in a few hundred stores with plans to expand.

**Refused angles**

- "Instacart's smart carts win the checkout-free race after rivals pulled back" — `hallucinated_fact`. No competitor, rival retreat, or category comparison is in the facts. Bring sourced competitor data and this becomes a counterposition angle.
- "Instacart's $1B ad business is now X% of the retail media market" — `hallucinated_fact`. The share figure requires market data the update does not contain.
- "Smart carts put ads in front of shoppers mid-aisle, raising privacy questions" — `hallucinated_fact`. Nothing in the update says the carts display ads or what shopper data they collect.
- "Instacart hits $1 billion advertising milestone" — `duplicate` of the advertiser angle, and written like a press release.
- "How Instacart's CEO turned a delivery app into a profitable ad platform" — `no_journalist_shape`. No executive is named and no operating detail is supplied. A profile needs a person.
- "Instacart proves gig delivery can be profitable" — `off-beat`. Profitability is stated, but nothing ties it to the delivery model, and the delivery segment is the slow part.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- What does "annual run rate" mean here: last month times twelve, or last quarter times four? A financial reporter will ask in the first reply.
- Is single-digit order growth a number you want in the same sentence as the ad milestone? The strongest angle depends on putting them together. If you will not confirm the growth figure on record, that angle shrinks.
- Can you name one grocery chain with carts live and give a store count? Without it, the in-store angle is a rollout press release.
- Do you have any data on checkout staffing from a live store? If the answer is no, decide now how the company responds when a labor reporter asks.
- Is the smart-cart expansion actually news today, or is the ad figure the news and the carts are riding along? A reporter will tell the two apart even if the announcement does not.
