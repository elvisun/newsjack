**Liquid Death sells in 120,000 stores. Now it wants one of its own.**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A beverage or CPG trade reporter covering challenger-brand channel strategy: how brands built on wholesale distribution decide to own a door. The tension is the number. A brand present in roughly 120,000 retail doors has no distribution problem to solve, so the question is what a single owned store is for: limited-edition sales channel, brand lab, event venue, or marketing expense with a cash register. Not for lifestyle or entertainment press, and not for general startup blogs that will just rewrite the announcement.
- **Why now:** The first permanent store is the company event. The channel-strategy question has a longer window if the company will say what it expects the store to do.
- **Decay:** week. The opening itself is a 24-hour item, but the wholesale-brand-goes-physical thesis carries a trend piece for a week if the founder supplies the reasoning.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up, plus the magnitude contrast between 120,000 doors and one. The store is evidence about a distribution strategy, not a retail object. The other kept angles drop down to the store's contents, over to the money, and over to the certification.
- **Proof it needs:** the opening date, address, and size; what the store is expected to sell that retail partners cannot; whether limited-edition cans are store-exclusive; a founder or retail lead willing to say on record what success looks like for the store.
- **Facts it rests on:** first permanent physical store, a flagship in Los Angeles; canned water sold in roughly 120,000 US retail doors; the store sells merch, limited-edition canned water, and runs in-store events.

**Liquid Death's first store is a merch shop that also sells water**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A marketing or brand-strategy trade reporter covering merch, limited drops, and brand experience as revenue lines rather than promotions. The entry point is the inventory list: merch, limited-edition cans, events. That is the shape of an apparel or entertainment brand, not a beverage aisle. The reporter can test whether a water company is operating as a lifestyle brand that happens to sell water, and what that means for merch as a business. Not for beverage distribution desks, finance reporters, or retail real-estate press.
- **Why now:** The store opening is the first physical proof of the merch-first mix. Without it, this is positioning, not news.
- **Decay:** week. The brand-as-merch-business piece runs off the opening but does not depend on the day.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, with the merch business as the protagonist. The first angle is about where the brand sells; this one is about what the brand is. It survives only if merch is a material revenue line.
- **Proof it needs:** merch revenue or share of sales, or at least unit volume; the SKU split in the store between merch and water; the event calendar with at least one confirmed event; whether limited-edition cans are priced as collectibles.
- **Facts it rests on:** the store sells merch, limited-edition canned water, and runs in-store events; first permanent physical store; no new product line announced.

**Liquid Death says it's profitable. The new store tests what that word covers.**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-brand finance reporter or a business-of-CPG newsletter writer who reads challenger-brand profitability claims for what they leave out. The hook is the disclosure gap: profitable on a contribution-margin basis, net profit undisclosed, and now a flagship store with fixed costs that sit below the contribution line. This reporter will ask whether the store is booked as marketing or as retail, and whether the company will ever say more than contribution margin. Not for retail-design trades, lifestyle press, or impact reporters.
- **Why now:** The store adds a new fixed-cost line at the exact moment the company is publicizing a partial profitability claim. That combination is the story, and it is live only while the opening is news.
- **Decay:** week. Unit-economics pieces run after the opening day, but the peg fades once the store is old news.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, money as the protagonist. It is the only angle that treats the announcement skeptically, and it is the one a finance reporter would write without being pitched.
- **Proof it needs:** a plain definition of what contribution-margin profitability includes and excludes, on record; whether the store is expected to be P&L-positive on its own; the store's budget classification (marketing or retail); any willingness to disclose net profit or an operating margin range. Without the first item, do not pitch this.
- **Facts it rests on:** the brand is profitable on a contribution-margin basis but has not disclosed net profit; first permanent physical store; canned water sold in roughly 120,000 retail doors.

**A canned-water brand in 120,000 stores became a B Corp. What did it have to change?**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** An impact-business or ESG trade reporter covering B Corp certification of scaled consumer brands, or a philanthropy trade reporter who covers corporate giving mechanisms. The certification is fresh this quarter and the company has a giving number attached. This reporter will want the certification to have cost something: a supply-chain change, a governance change, a can-sourcing commitment. If it changed nothing, the story is a press release. Not for beverage distribution desks, finance press, or retail trades.
- **Why now:** Certification landed this quarter, which ends June 30. The store opening gives a second reason to write about the company in the same window.
- **Decay:** week. The certification is recent enough to be a hook now. By July it is background.
- **What makes it distinct:** Two lenses, rules and data, with the company's accountability claims as the protagonist. Every other angle is about commerce; this one is about whether the impact claims survive a specific question.
- **Proof it needs:** the B Corp score and the assessment areas where the company changed practices to qualify; the $4.2M broken out by recipient and by year, with the giving mechanism (fixed percentage of sales or discretionary); at least one named nonprofit recipient willing to confirm; context for $4.2M against revenue, which the company has not supplied.
- **Facts it rests on:** the company became a Certified B Corp this quarter; the company says it has donated $4.2M to clean-water nonprofits since founding; canned water sold in roughly 120,000 US retail doors.

**Refused angles**

- "Liquid Death opens LA flagship" as a local business-desk item: `duplicate`. Same protagonist as the first angle with thinner proof. No address, no opening date, no headcount, no neighborhood detail was supplied.
- "$4.2M to clean-water nonprofits" as a standalone philanthropy story: `duplicate`. Same accountability protagonist as the B Corp angle. The number without the certification is a press release line.
- "Liquid Death proves physical retail works for digital-first brands": `hallucinated_fact`. The store has no results yet, and no comparison data was supplied.
- "A brand growing without new SKUs cuts against the CPG playbook": `hallucinated_fact`. A contrarian angle needs a documented prevailing belief and evidence against it. "No new product line announced" is an absence, not evidence.
- "Liquid Death's Country Club redefines experiential retail": `slop`. Rewritten once into the first angle. The original framing is a press release headline.
- "Country Club events as a music or nightlife story": `off-beat`. No event, performer, or programming was named. An entertainment desk has nothing to write.
- Any news-peg angle: `no_why_now_but_required`. No current signal was supplied and none was invented.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- When does the store open, where exactly, and how large is it? Two of the four angles have no clock without an opening date.
- What does "profitable on a contribution-margin basis" exclude? If the answer is marketing, and the store is marketing, the finance angle turns against you.
- Is the store expected to make money, or is it a marketing line item with a register? Decide before a reporter asks.
- What share of revenue is merch? If you will not say, the merch angle is a hunch a reporter will discount.
- What did B Corp certification require you to change? If the answer is nothing, do not pitch the impact angle.
- Who received the $4.2M, over what period, and by what mechanism? Is it large relative to revenue, or small?
- Does "Country Club" imply a membership with terms? If so, that is a fact reporters will find, so supply it first.

**Next step:** `journalist-fit-check`. Four shapes exist, but each carries a proof gap that decides whether it is real. Pick one, close its gap with the questions above, then resolve real names for that one angle only.
