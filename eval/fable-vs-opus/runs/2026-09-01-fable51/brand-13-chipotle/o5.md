**Chipotle's avocado robot arrives at 100 stores as the chain freezes menu prices**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A restaurant-industry trade reporter covering unit economics and back-of-house automation — the kind who writes about how chains absorb food-cost inflation without a price hike. The story isn't the robot; it's the pairing. Chipotle is publicly holding prices flat for a year while pushing automation into 100 kitchens, which invites a reporter to test whether one is paying for the other. Not for consumer food bloggers, gadget desks, or general AI reporters who'd cover the robot as a novelty.
- **Why now:** Both the 100-restaurant rollout and the price-freeze commitment are live company news as of today; the two statements were made together and can only be examined together right now.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard company-news window. The margin question survives longer, but the reporter's reason to call today is the announcement.
- **What makes it distinct:** Relevance + conflict news-value lens, with the company's cost structure as protagonist. Every other kept angle takes a different protagonist — the crew, the category, the guacamole itself.
- **Proof it needs:** the per-restaurant cost of an Autocado unit and the payback period; whether the price freeze is funded by automation savings or by something else (menu mix, supplier contracts, accepting thinner margins); the labor-cost figures currently undisclosed; whether "flat prices" covers all 3,500+ locations or only some markets; the exact term of the freeze (calendar year, fiscal year, twelve rolling months).
- **Facts it rests on:** Autocado is rolling out to 100 restaurants; Chipotle says it will not raise menu prices in the coming year despite food-cost inflation; the chain has over 3,500 locations; labor-cost figures not disclosed.

**What happens to the crew when the guac machine shows up**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A labor reporter at a restaurant-workforce or service-sector-labor outlet covering what automation actually does to hourly schedules. Chipotle is making an explicit, checkable promise — no job cuts, staff redeployed to the front line — at a scale (100 stores now, 3,500+ possible) where a reporter can go back in six months and see whether it held. "Redeployed" is the word a labor reporter will want to unpack: same hours, same pay, different station, or fewer hours per store? Not for tech desks covering robotics, and not for investor-facing outlets that will read it purely as a margin story.
- **Why now:** The no-cuts commitment is being made today, alongside a rollout number that makes it testable. A promise is most reportable at the moment it's made and before it's been tested.
- **Decay:** week — the commitment is news today, but the labor framing supports a reported piece over the next several days, and the follow-up lives months out.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the worker as protagonist, not the company or the customer. The counterposition angle asks who pays for the price freeze; this one asks what "redeployed to the front line" means for a specific person's shift.
- **Proof it needs:** whether total store labor hours change, not just headcount; what "front line" roles the redeployed staff move into and at what pay; a crew member or general manager from a pilot kitchen who can speak on record; whether the no-cuts pledge is time-bound or open-ended; the withheld labor-cost figures, or an on-record explanation of why they're withheld.
- **Facts it rests on:** no jobs will be cut, the company says, with staff redeployed to the front line; Autocado is rolling out to 100 restaurants; the chain has over 3,500 locations; labor-cost figures not disclosed.

**Two robots, one bowl: Chipotle is automating the assembly line from both ends**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A food-technology or restaurant-operations reporter at a trade outlet covering kitchen automation as a buildout, not a gimmick. The hook is that Autocado (prep) and the Digital Makeline (assembly) attack opposite ends of the same line — that's an architecture, and it's the first evidence that a major chain is automating a full assembly-line format rather than bolting on one machine. Other operators watching this decide their own capex from it. Not for consumer lifestyle press or general business desks that will flatten it to "Chipotle has robots."
- **Why now:** Autocado is moving from pilot to 100 restaurants while the Digital Makeline is still in testing — that gap between one system graduating and the other not yet is the reportable moment, and it closes as soon as the Makeline's status changes.
- **Decay:** week — the two-system architecture is a context piece a trade reporter can develop past announcement day.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — the story is the automated assembly-line format across the category, not the avocado machine or the individual worker. The counterposition angle is about cost; this one is about how a fast-casual kitchen gets rebuilt.
- **Proof it needs:** how many restaurants have the Digital Makeline and whether it has a rollout timeline at all; how the two systems interact on a single line, if they do; whether both were built in-house or with named partners; what the 100 stores were selected for (volume, format, geography); throughput figures beyond guacamole.
- **Facts it rests on:** Chipotle is rolling out Autocado to 100 restaurants; it is also testing a robotic bowl-assembly system called the Digital Makeline; Autocado roughly halves guacamole prep time in pilot kitchens.

**Half the prep time: what a guacamole robot actually does to a lunch rush**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A restaurant-operations reporter covering throughput, wait times, and service speed at a trade or business outlet — the desk that treats prep minutes as a real operating metric. "Roughly halves guacamole prep time" is the only performance number in the announcement, and it's the one a reporter can pressure-test: halved from what baseline, measured over how many kitchens, and does saved prep time show up as shorter lines or just as redeployed hours? Not for AI or robotics reporters, who want the machine; this beat wants the minutes.
- **Why now:** The number is being attached to a 100-restaurant commitment today, which is what makes a single pilot statistic worth interrogating.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the metric is only news while the rollout is. After that it becomes background for someone else's story.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, scale and contextualization — the protagonist is the number itself, and the honest version of this angle is as much about what the number doesn't say as what it does. Distinct from the counterposition angle, which never has to defend the prep figure, and from the labor angle, which is about people rather than minutes.
- **Proof it needs:** the baseline prep time and the post-Autocado time in real minutes; how many pilot kitchens produced the "roughly halves" figure and over what period; whether it was measured by Chipotle or independently; guacamole volume per store per day; whether saved prep time converts into measurable service-speed change. Without the baseline, "roughly halves" is a marketing number and a good reporter will treat it as one.
- **Facts it rests on:** Autocado roughly halves guacamole prep time in pilot kitchens; rollout to 100 restaurants.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Are you actually authorized to speak for Chipotle here, or is this a client brief about a company you don't represent? Every angle above assumes you can put a Chipotle operator, engineer, or crew member on the phone. If you can't, all four collapse into commentary, and that's a different skill.
- Why are labor-cost figures undisclosed? Every reporter on the labor angle will ask, and "not disclosed" is itself the story to some of them. Decide the on-record answer before pitching, not during the interview.
- Does the price freeze have a written term and a scope? "The coming year" and "not raise menu prices" are both softer than they sound. A reporter will ask about regional pricing, delivery markups, portion sizes, and third-party app pricing. If any of those move, the freeze headline becomes a correction.
- What is the baseline behind "roughly halves"? If nobody can produce the before-number, drop the data angle rather than let a reporter discover the gap.
- Are the 100 restaurants a real rollout or an extended pilot? "Rolling out to 100" out of 3,500+ is under 3% of the estate. If there's no commitment beyond those 100, the category-creation angle is overclaiming and should be pitched as a test, not a rollout.

If food-cost inflation, restaurant labor, or automation is live in the news this week, the counterposition angle gets materially stronger — but I'm not going to assert that it is.
