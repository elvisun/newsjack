**Whoop puts a cleared ECG and an uncleared blood-pressure estimate on the same wrist**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A digital-health regulatory reporter at a health-policy or medtech trade outlet covering where the FDA's line between "wellness" and "device" actually sits. The hook is the split inside one launch: the ECG went through clearance for atrial fibrillation detection, while the blood-pressure feature ships as a non-medical estimate that needs a cuff to calibrate. That is a live regulatory question with a fresh example attached. Not for gadget reviewers, fitness lifestyle press, or general startup desks.
- **Why now:** The launch is live now and gives a concrete, current example of the wellness-label posture. The underlying regulatory question has a longer runway.
- **Decay:** week. The launch is a 24-hour event, but the regulatory framing supports a context piece for several days.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, regulator protagonist, zoomed up to the category. The story is the rulebook, not the product or the price.
- **Proof it needs:** the FDA clearance letter or listing for the ECG feature; Whoop's own wording of the blood-pressure disclaimer; a plain answer on whether Whoop intends to seek clearance for blood pressure or treat "wellness" as permanent; what the one-time cuff calibration involves and whether it expires.
- **Facts it rests on:** ECG received FDA clearance for detecting atrial fibrillation; blood-pressure insights launch as a non-medical wellness estimate, not a diagnostic; calibration requires a cuff; a cardiologist advisory board reviewed the features.

**What Whoop's cardiologists actually signed off on**

- **Why a journalist cares:** A cardiology or clinical-technology trade reporter covering consumer AFib screening and the downstream load it puts on clinics: false positives, worried-well referrals, and estimates patients mistake for readings. Whoop is offering a cardiologist advisory board as reassurance. The reporter will want the validation numbers behind that reassurance, not the board's existence. Not for gadget reviewers, business desks, or general consumer press.

- **Decay:** week. Clinical-evidence pieces run after launch day if the data is real and quotable.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, clinician protagonist, zoomed down to accuracy. The regulatory angle is about labels; this one is about numbers and what happens to a patient after an alert.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- What does the top tier cost, and what does a current member on a lower tier have to pay to get AFib detection? The paywall angle is the sharpest here and it cannot be pitched without the number.
- Does Whoop supply the cuff or does the member buy one? "Pay for the top tier, then buy a cuff" is the line a reporter will write if you do not answer it first.
- What are the AFib sensitivity and specificity figures, and what is the blood-pressure estimate's error against a cuff? If you cannot produce them, the clinical angle is gone and the regulatory angle weakens.
- Is "wellness" a permanent posture for blood pressure, or a stopgap while Whoop seeks clearance? The answer changes the regulatory story completely.
- Which rivals already ship cleared ECG, and how long ago? If the gap is years, the catch-up angle is the story whether you pitch it or not.
- Will any advisory-board cardiologist go on record, and were they paid? A paid, unnamed board is a liability in a pitch, not an asset.
- Is the ECG clearance limited to the newest band? Existing members on older hardware are a follow-up story you should expect.
