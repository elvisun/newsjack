**Your mattress now watches your blood oxygen. Doctors have questions.**

- **Story type:** defensive-comment
- **Why a journalist cares:** A health technology reporter covering the regulatory boundary between consumer wellness devices and FDA-cleared medical diagnostics — at STAT News, KHN, Wired Health, or the health desk at a major daily. SpO2 detection is clinically meaningful: low overnight blood oxygen is the primary marker of sleep apnea, which affects tens of millions of adults, most undiagnosed. A mattress that flags oxygen dips while explicitly not being a diagnostic device creates a patient-safety tension that a health reporter can build a story around. Not for: consumer product reviewers, sports recovery press, or general tech product desks.
- **Why now:** The feature launched now. Consumer SpO2 monitoring has been a live regulatory and clinical controversy since wrist-based devices added it — this extends the debate to a new form factor with different accuracy questions.
- **Decay:** week — the launch is the 24-hour peg, but the regulatory and patient-safety analysis can sustain a week-long angle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, regulator/patient-safety protagonist. The story isn't the feature — it's the gap between what the feature detects (blood oxygen, a clinically significant signal) and what the company is legally allowed to claim (wellness only). Structurally different from the category and business-model angles below, which don't foreground the safety question.
- **Proof it needs:** An on-record sleep medicine physician explaining the clinical implications of non-FDA-cleared SpO2 monitoring for undiagnosed sleep apnea patients; accuracy specification for mattress-based vs. clinical-grade SpO2 measurement; the company's exact product copy on how it positions the feature versus a sleep study.
- **Facts it rests on:** Features track SpO2 and snoring overnight; features are wellness, not FDA-cleared diagnostics; the clinical study with a named sleep clinic is ongoing with no results yet.

## Uncomfortable questions

- **What does the clinical study actually look like?** "Partnering with a sleep clinic" can mean anything from rigorous IRB-approved research to an in-house study the company designed and funded. Until you can name the clinic, the design, the funding structure, and the publication plan, the study is not a proof point — it's a liability if a reporter digs in.
- **What is the SpO2 accuracy specification from a mattress?** Blood-oxygen sensing under a sleeping body is mechanically different from a finger-clip oximeter or wrist sensor. If you don't know the accuracy range, a health reporter will — and the first question they ask will expose the gap.
- **Has legal reviewed the snoring detection messaging in the context of sleep apnea?** Snoring plus SpO2 dips is the clinical presentation of obstructive sleep apnea. Detecting both on a wellness device without steering users toward clinical evaluation is the exact fact pattern that draws FDA warning letters. You should know where the line is before you pitch a health reporter.
- **Is any customer willing to go on record?** Three of the four kept angles become significantly stronger with a named user — especially an athlete or biohacker — who can describe what the feature actually tells them and how they act on it.
