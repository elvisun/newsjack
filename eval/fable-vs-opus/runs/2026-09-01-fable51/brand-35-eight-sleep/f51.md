**Eight Sleep's mattress now tracks blood oxygen and snoring. It won't call the result a diagnosis.**

- **Story type:** defensive-comment
- **Why a journalist cares:** A digital-health regulatory reporter at a health-tech trade or a health desk that covers the wellness-versus-medical-device line. Overnight SpO2 and snoring are the kind of signals clinicians use, and a consumer company shipping them under a wellness label is exactly the boundary this beat tests. The reporter's first question is what the app tells a user whose oxygen drops overnight. Not for gadget reviewers, sports-performance outlets, or startup-business desks.
- **Why now:** The features are launching now. The question exists the moment the reading appears on a customer's phone.
- **Decay:** week. The launch is a 24-hour event, but the wellness-label question stays live as long as the feature is new.
- **What makes it distinct:** Regulator lens. The protagonist is the rule, not the product or the price. The other kept angles are about the business model, the product category, and the clinic.
- **Proof it needs:** The exact in-app and marketing language describing what the SpO2 and snoring readings are and are not. A spokesperson willing to answer on record what a user is told when overnight SpO2 is low. The company's position on whether it intends to pursue FDA clearance. The basis for treating the features as general wellness.
- **Facts it rests on:** The new sensors track SpO2 and snoring overnight. The features are wellness, not FDA-cleared diagnostics. A clinical study is ongoing with no results.

**A bed that costs several thousand dollars. The health sensors are behind the top subscription tier.**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-tech business reporter covering hardware subscription economics and software-locked features on owned devices. This is a concrete, current example of a premium hardware company gating new sensor capability behind a paid tier, and the reporter can ask what a lower-tier owner actually gets. Also fits a consumer-advocacy or personal-finance columnist who covers subscription creep. Not for sleep-medicine or regulatory desks; they will read the price as a distraction.
- **Why now:** The gating decision ships with the launch. It is only news while the tier structure is new.
- **Decay:** week. Subscription-model stories run past launch day if the pricing details are specific.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the buyer's money as protagonist. Nothing about medical claims or product category. It is a story about who pays for what.
- **Proof it needs:** Pricing for every subscription tier and what each includes. Whether existing cover owners need new hardware, the top tier, or both to get these features. Whether the sensors are physically new or were already in the cover and are now unlocked. A company statement on why the features are tier-gated.
- **Facts it rests on:** The features are software-gated behind the top subscription tier. The product costs several thousand dollars. Eight Sleep is privately held.

**Sleep trackers tell you that you snored. This bed changes its temperature while you are doing it.**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-health or sleep-tech product reporter at a consumer tech outlet or a health-and-wellness publication that follows the shift from passive tracking to devices that act on what they measure. The distinction is that a wearable reports in the morning and this product responds during the night. Not for regulatory reporters, who get the first angle, and not for business desks, who get the second.
- **Why now:** The closed-loop behavior ships with the launch.
- **Decay:** week. The launch peg lasts a day; the detect-and-respond framing holds about a week before it becomes a feature review.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up to the category. The protagonist is the product's action, not its label or its price. It is the only angle where the automatic temperature adjustment is the story.
- **Proof it needs:** What the temperature response actually does: which direction, by how much, on what trigger. Any internal data on whether the change affects snoring or SpO2. If there is none, say so before pitching; the reporter will ask, and the angle survives as detect-and-respond, not as improvement. A live demo or a screen recording of the overnight response.
- **Facts it rests on:** The sensors track SpO2 and snoring overnight and adjust the bed's temperature automatically. The company markets to athletes and biohackers.

**A sleep clinic agreed to study a consumer mattress. Here is what it is measuring.**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A sleep-medicine trade reporter or clinical-newsletter writer who follows consumer-device validation studies. The clinic is the protagonist: why it agreed, what endpoints it chose, and what it will and will not conclude about a wellness product. Not for consumer tech, business, or lifestyle desks. This angle is thin until the clinic speaks. Without the clinic on record, it is a press-release line and should not go out.
- **Why now:** The launch is the only current peg. The real story lands when results do, and there are none yet.
- **Decay:** month. Nothing about the study is time-pressured today; the launch gives it a soft reason to run now.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, clinic as protagonist. The other angles are about the company, the buyer, and the rule. This one is about an outside institution putting its name on the product.
- **Proof it needs:** The clinic's principal investigator on record. Study design, endpoints, sample size, and duration. Whether the study is registered. Who is funding it and whether the clinic has any financial relationship with Eight Sleep. What "partnership" means in practice.
- **Facts it rests on:** The clinical study with a named sleep clinic is ongoing with no results yet. The features are wellness, not FDA-cleared diagnostics.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- What does the app show a user whose overnight SpO2 drops? Does it say anything about seeing a doctor? A health reporter asks this first, and the answer decides whether the wellness label holds.
- Does an existing cover owner need new hardware, the top tier, or both? If the answer is both, the subscription angle gets sharper and less flattering.
- Will the clinic's investigator go on record with design, endpoints, sample size, and funding? If not, drop the clinic angle entirely.
- Is there any evidence that the automatic temperature change does anything for snoring or blood oxygen? If not, every angle must stay at "detects and responds," never "improves."
- You market to athletes and biohackers. Snoring and overnight blood oxygen are signals a different population tends to worry about. Who are these features actually for, and does the marketing match the product?
- Is the launch public as of now, or under embargo? Decay tags above assume it is live today.
