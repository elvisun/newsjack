**Databricks says revenue run rate passed $3B, growing 60%, with an IPO still undated**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A pre-IPO markets reporter at a financial outlet or a VC-and-late-stage newsletter tracking which private AI companies are disclosing numbers ahead of a listing. The hook is a private company volunteering a revenue figure and a growth rate while holding a $62B mark and hinting at an IPO. This reporter will test whether the numbers support the valuation and read the disclosure as IPO groundwork. Not for AI research press, developer outlets, or regulated-industry trade reporters who don't cover capital markets.
- **Why now:** The revenue disclosure is being made today, and the company paired it with a signaled but undated IPO.
- **Decay:** 24hr. A run-rate disclosure is standard same-day company news; the IPO context extends it only if there is a filing or a date, and there is neither.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, money as protagonist. The story is the company's financial trajectory and listing readiness, not the model, not the buyers, not the vendors it competes with.
- **Proof it needs:** how "annualized" is calculated (monthly or quarterly run rate, GAAP or not); the date and round of the $62B valuation; an on-record executive statement on the IPO signal rather than an inference; the prior-year comparison figure behind the 60% growth claim.
- **Facts it rests on:** crossed $3B in annualized revenue; revenue grew over 60% year over year; privately held, last valued at $62B; the company has signaled an eventual IPO without a date.

**Databricks takes aim at closed AI APIs with an open model for regulated buyers**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** An enterprise-AI procurement reporter at a B2B technology trade outlet, or a vertical trade reporter covering technology decisions in regulated industries, who tracks the open-model versus closed-API buying debate. The hook is a direct challenge to closed-API vendors on the ground where they are most vulnerable: buyers who cannot send data outside their own environment. This reporter can call closed-API vendors for response and call regulated buyers to ask whether the pitch matches their actual constraints. Not for IPO or markets reporters, consumer AI press, or general startup blogs.
- **Why now:** The model and its license are released today; the counterposition is live while closed-API vendors are being asked to respond.
- **Decay:** week. The launch itself is a 24-hour event, but a vendor-comparison piece with buyer and competitor reaction can run through the week.
- **What makes it distinct:** Conflict lens, incumbent closed-API vendors as the counterparty. The first angle is about the balance sheet and the third is about the business model; this one is about who loses a customer if the pitch works.
- **Proof it needs:** the exact license terms (commercial use, redistribution, any usage restrictions) so the "open" claim survives scrutiny; at least one regulated enterprise willing to say on record why closed APIs did not work for them; a concrete statement of what "fine-tune on their own data" means operationally versus what closed-API vendors offer; independent benchmark results, since the company's own numbers will not carry a comparison story.
- **Facts it rests on:** the model is released under an open license for enterprises to fine-tune on their own data; the company positions it as an alternative to closed APIs for regulated enterprises; benchmark numbers are the company's own.

**Why a company with $3B in revenue is giving its foundation model away**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A business-of-AI reporter at a business publication or an AI-industry newsletter covering how open models get paid for. The hook is the pairing: a large revenue disclosure and an open-license model release on the same day. This reporter wants to know what the open model does for the business, whether it pulls enterprises onto paid products or is a cost the company absorbs for positioning. Not for compliance trade reporters, IPO desks, or developer outlets that only cover model capabilities.
- **Why now:** The two facts arrived together today. The pairing is the news; separately, each is weaker.
- **Decay:** week. Business-model analysis pieces run after the launch day if the company supplies a real answer to how the model relates to revenue.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up to the category. The protagonist is the open-model business model, not Databricks' numbers and not its competitors. It is only a story if the company can explain the link between an open release and paid revenue; if it can't, this collapses back into the first angle.
- **Proof it needs:** an on-record explanation of how the open model is expected to drive revenue; any evidence of what enterprises pay for once they adopt the model; the license terms, since a restrictive license changes the story from "giving it away" to something else.
- **Facts it rests on:** the model is released under an open license; crossed $3B in annualized revenue; revenue grew over 60% year over year; the company positions the model as an alternative to closed APIs for regulated enterprises.

**Refused angles**

- "Databricks' open model proves open source now beats closed models" — `hallucinated_fact`. The benchmark numbers are the company's own. No independent evaluation was supplied, so the comparison claim cannot be made.
- "Regulated enterprises are already fine-tuning Databricks' model on their own data" — `hallucinated_fact`. No customer is named. A customer-story with no customer is an invitation to invent one.
- "Data sovereignty: compliance teams get an AI model that keeps data in-house" — `duplicate`. Same positioning sentence, same buyer, same counterparty as the counterposition angle. It only becomes a separate compliance story with a named regulated customer or a regulator's own statement.
- "Databricks' open model is a game-changer for enterprise AI" — `slop`. Rewrite attempt produced the counterposition angle above; the original phrasing is dead.
- "Databricks is the next big AI IPO" — `no_why_now_but_required`. There is no date, no filing, and no on-record timeline. "Eventual IPO" is not a peg.
- "What Databricks' $62B valuation says about the AI market" — `hallucinated_fact`. The valuation date is unknown and there is no supplied market comparison to build a data angle on.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- How is "$3B annualized" calculated? A markets reporter will ask whether it is a monthly or quarterly run rate and whether it is GAAP. If you can't answer, the first angle dies on the first call.
- When was the $62B valuation set? If it is a year old, the story becomes "has the price moved," which you may not want.
- What does the "open license" actually permit? If it restricts commercial use or redistribution, "open-sourcing" in a headline will get challenged, and the counterposition angle weakens.
- Will you publish independent benchmark results or allow a third party to evaluate the model? Company-run benchmarks are the first thing a skeptical reporter flags, and they cannot anchor any comparison to closed APIs.
- Can a single regulated enterprise be named, even as an evaluator? Without one, the regulated-buyer positioning is a claim, not a story.
- Is there an on-record explanation of how the open model connects to revenue? Without it, the trend angle is a question you are asking reporters to answer for you.
- Is the IPO signal an executive quote or your inference from public remarks? Reporters will want the quote.
