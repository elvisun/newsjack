**Klarna's AI does the work of 700 agents. Its headcount fell 20 percent.**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A labor-economics reporter on a business daily's workforce desk, or a workforce-data reporter at an HR/employment trade, who covers white-collar automation and works from company-disclosed headcount numbers rather than vendor forecasts. Most AI-and-jobs stories are projections; this is a named company putting a workload equivalent and a headcount change on the record in the same breath. Not for consumer tech desks, product reviewers, or AI-model press — they want the model, not the payroll.
- **Why now:** The numbers are being disclosed now, and a company that has filed to go public is about to have its cost structure read closely by people who do this for a living.
- **Decay:** week — the disclosure is the trigger, but a labor reporter needs a few days to pull comparators and get a union or labour-economist voice in.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens (scale plus change over time), with the workforce as the subject. The other angles put the investor, the consumer, and the remaining human support team in the protagonist seat.
- **Proof it needs:** the split between roles cut and roles left unfilled — without it this angle is unpitchable, because it is the first question and you currently refuse to answer it; how "the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents" was calculated (chats per agent per hour, over what period, against what baseline); the start and end dates of the 20 percent headcount decline; whether the two-thirds figure is share of chats or share of resolved chats.
- **Facts it rests on:** the assistant handles two-thirds of customer-service chats; it handled the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents in its first months; most hiring was frozen and headcount fell about 20 percent over the year, partly through attrition.

**Klarna heads for an IPO with two-thirds of its support desk automated**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A fintech or payments reporter at a financial trade or markets desk who reads S-1-stage filings and writes about unit economics — cost-to-serve per user, support cost as a share of opex, what a profitability claim actually rests on. The hook is not the chatbot; it is whether an automated support function is a durable margin story or a one-off cost cut that annualises away. Not for general startup press, consumer money columnists, or AI-industry newsletters.
- **Why now:** The company is profitable and has filed to go public, so its cost structure is now a live question for people pricing it.
- **Decay:** 24hr — filing-adjacent economics is a same-cycle story for a markets desk; once other outlets have written the filing's numbers, a follow-on pitch reads as catch-up.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the money as protagonist, at the top of the ladder — this is about what the automation does to the P&L, not about jobs or about consumers.
- **Proof it needs:** securities counsel clearance before a single number goes to a reporter — you have filed to go public and selective disclosure of financial metrics is not a PR decision; support cost per user before and after; whether the 20 percent headcount decline shows up in opex or was offset by hiring elsewhere; whether "profitable" means net income, adjusted, or a segment.
- **Facts it rests on:** Klarna is profitable and has filed to go public; the assistant handles two-thirds of customer-service chats; average resolution time fell from 11 minutes to under 2; headcount fell about 20 percent; payments processed for 150 million consumers.

**Klarna automated two-thirds of its chats. The other third got harder.**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A support-operations or customer-experience trade reporter covering contact-centre staffing, escalation design, and what happens to a support team when the easy tickets disappear. The prevailing belief is that AI deflection makes support cheaper and simpler across the board; the fact set undercuts it — the machine takes the two-thirds that were resolvable in minutes, and the humans keep a caseload that is now entirely the hard third. That changes hiring profile, training, burnout and staffing math in a way nobody publishes numbers on. Not for policy desks, markets reporters, or AI-model coverage.
- **Why now:** The two-thirds threshold is newly disclosed, which is the first point at which the residual-caseload question becomes concrete rather than theoretical.
- **Decay:** week — an operations trend piece, not an announcement; it survives past the news cycle if you can supply the composition data.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens, with the remaining human agents as protagonist. Angle one counts the people who left; this one is about the people who stayed and what their day now looks like.
- **Proof it needs:** what the residual third actually contains, by category; average handle time for human-handled chats before and after the assistant launched (if it rose, that is the story, and you should know before a reporter asks); any change in agent role level, pay band, or training; attrition rate inside the support org specifically, separate from the company-wide 20 percent.
- **Facts it rests on:** the assistant handles two-thirds of customer-service chats, which leaves a third that it does not; average resolution time fell from 11 minutes to under 2; headcount fell about 20 percent, partly through attrition.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- You are declining to say how many roles were cut versus left unfilled, and you are pitching a story whose central number is a headcount decline. Every reporter worth targeting will ask that in the first reply. Decide the answer before you send anything, because "we won't say" printed next to "700 full-time agents" is a worse story than the split, whatever the split is.
- You have filed to go public. Who has cleared these metrics for public disclosure? Talking to press about resolution times, headcount and profitability during a filing window is a legal question before it is a PR one. If counsel has not signed off, none of these angles goes out.
