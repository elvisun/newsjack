**Warby Parker's new stores are optometry clinics that happen to sell frames**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** An optical/eyecare trade reporter, or a retail-health reporter at a healthcare business outlet, covering the shift of routine vision care out of independent optometry practices and into national retail chains. The hook isn't the store count — it's that most of the 40 new locations are staffed by optometrists doing comprehensive exams, which makes Warby a healthcare provider footprint story, not a DTC-glasses story. Not for consumer shopping desks, DTC/e-commerce trade press, or general startup outlets.
- **Why now:** The 40-store expansion and the exam rollout are being announced now, and the 300-store threshold gives the reporter a concrete marker to hang the trend on.
- **Decay:** week — the announcement is a 24-hour event, but the "retail chains are absorbing routine eye care" thesis holds a reporter's interest for longer.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — the story is the category (retail-delivered vision care), not the company. The other kept angles drop down to the P&L, over to the pharmacy partner, and sideways to the independent optometrists on the other end.
- **Proof it needs:** how many of the 40 stores will actually have an optometrist and on what timeline; whether Warby employs the ODs or leases to independent practices (this determines the regulatory story in states with corporate-practice-of-optometry rules); how many exams the existing fleet performs; whether exams take insurance and which plans.
- **Facts it rests on:** 40 new stores; store count passing 300; most new stores will offer optometrist-staffed comprehensive eye exams, not just glasses.

**A profitable Warby Parker won't say what its eye exams earn**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** An equity/retail-earnings reporter or a sell-side-facing markets desk covering newly profitable consumer names and the quality of that profitability. Warby is public and recently profitable, it is spending on 40 stores plus clinical staffing, and it is declining to break out exam revenue — that gap is the story for someone who reads segment disclosure for a living. Not for lifestyle, fashion, or consumer product-review outlets.
- **Why now:** The expansion commits capital now, and the disclosure gap is live now: the company is asking the market to fund a clinical build-out it doesn't separately report.
- **Decay:** 24hr for the announcement peg; the disclosure question resets at the next earnings call, so a reporter may want to hold it for that.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens (magnitude plus the thing that *isn't* disclosed) with the investor as protagonist — this is a financials story, structurally opposite to the category piece and the optometrist piece.
- **Proof it needs:** the capex per store and the incremental cost of optometrist staffing; whether exam revenue will be broken out in future filings and, if not, why; the actual gap between retail and online-only customer spend, with the definition used (basket size? lifetime value? attach rate?) and the period measured; whether exam patients convert to frame buyers at a measurable rate. Without the spend figure sourced, this angle is not pitchable — that claim is currently a company assertion, not a disclosed number.
- **Facts it rests on:** publicly traded and recently reached profitability; 40 new stores with optometrist staffing; the company says retail customers spend more than online-only customers; exam-revenue contribution not broken out.

**The DTC brand that proved you don't need stores now has 300 of them**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail-strategy reporter at a business or retail trade outlet who has spent a decade covering the direct-to-consumer thesis — that digitally native brands could skip physical retail and win on customer acquisition economics. Warby was the canonical case study for that thesis. Passing 300 stores while saying retail customers outspend online-only ones is evidence against the belief this reporter has been reporting on. Not for e-commerce tooling press or growth-marketing outlets that want the tactics rather than the reversal.
- **Why now:** The 300-store threshold plus the profitability milestone lands the reversal at a countable number; the honest framing is "the case study updated," not breaking news.
- **Decay:** week — a thesis-reversal piece needs reporting time and doesn't die with the press release.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — it takes the prevailing DTC belief and uses the company's own numbers against it. The category angle asks where vision care is going; this one asks what the last decade of DTC coverage got wrong.
- **Proof it needs:** the retail-versus-online spend comparison as an actual figure with a stated methodology, not a company adjective; store-level economics or payback period; what share of revenue now comes through stores versus the site; a founder or exec willing to say on record that the original online-only thesis was incomplete. Without an executive who will say that plainly, this is a reporter's story about Warby, not Warby's story — and it may not flatter you.
- **Facts it rests on:** store count passing 300; publicly traded and recently reached profitability; the company says retail customers spend more than online-only customers.

**Warby Parker is testing whether glasses belong next to the pharmacy counter**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A drugstore/pharmacy-retail trade reporter or a mass-retail channel reporter covering how chains use floor space and third-party brands to defend traffic. The pilot puts a vertically integrated brand into someone else's store at the same moment it is building 40 of its own — that tension is the reportable question, and it touches the pharmacy chain's strategy as much as Warby's. Not for the optical trade desk already receiving the exam angle, and not for consumer-deals coverage.
- **Why now:** The pilot is disclosed now, and it sits alongside an owned-store expansion — a reporter can ask both parties which channel they actually believe in. The honest caveat: it's a pilot, so there may be no scale, no named partner, and no results to report yet.
- **Decay:** 24hr as an announcement item; upgrade only when the partner is named and terms are describable, which is when the story actually exists.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the channel partner as protagonist — the pharmacy chain's motivation is the story, which is a different beat and a different newsroom from the eyecare, financial, and DTC angles.
- **Proof it needs:** whether the pharmacy chain can be named and will comment; how many doors, which markets, what the assortment and price points are; whether exams are part of the pharmacy pilot or frames only; how this is priced and who owns the customer relationship. If the partner is unnamed and silent, do not pitch this — an anonymous pilot is a press release, not a story.
- **Facts it rests on:** piloting a partnership to sell glasses through a national pharmacy chain; 40 new stores; most new stores offer optometrist-staffed exams.

**What 40 chain optometry clinics mean for the independent eye doctor down the street**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A healthcare-workforce or independent-practice reporter — the kind who covers private-equity roll-ups and corporate consolidation in dentistry, dermatology, and veterinary care — plus optometry trade press covering practice ownership. Independent optometry is the classic small-practice profession, and a public company adding clinical capacity at scale is the same consolidation story they already cover in adjacent specialties. Not for retail or consumer desks, and not for anyone you want writing a friendly expansion story: this one has a protagonist who may not be you.
- **Why now:** The clinical hiring is happening now with the 40-store build; the consolidation question is a longer-running thread this expansion feeds.
- **Decay:** month — a structural workforce story with no urgent event behind it beyond the expansion itself.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the incumbent — the independent practitioner — as protagonist, and a bad-news framing of the same fact the category angle frames as growth. Different beat, different newsroom, opposite sympathies.
- **Proof it needs:** how many optometrists Warby is hiring and where; employment model (W-2, contracted, or independent practices subleasing space); pay and scope compared with private practice; the actual store locations, since the whole story depends on whether they cluster in markets with existing independents. You will also need to decide whether you want this story reported at all — a reporter will call the optometric associations for the other side.
- **Facts it rests on:** 40 new stores; most will offer optometrist-staffed comprehensive eye exams; store count passing 300; publicly traded and recently profitable.

- "Warby Parker hits 300 stores" — `no_journalist_shape`. A count is a milestone, not a story; every kept angle above uses the number as evidence for something, not as the news.

- "Eye exams are the future of eyewear retail" — `duplicate` of the category-creation angle, with the specifics removed.

- "Why Warby Parker's expansion matters for the future of work" — `off-beat`.
- "Warby Parker challenges Luxottica's dominance" — `hallucinated_fact`. No competitor is named anywhere in your facts and no market-share evidence was supplied. With a real share figure and a competitor response, this becomes a legitimate counterposition angle.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- What exactly does "retail customers spend more than online-only customers" measure — basket size, annual spend, lifetime value? Over what period, and is it selection effect? A reporter will ask, and if the answer is "customers near our stores were already higher-value," two of the angles above weaken considerably.
- Why isn't exam revenue broken out? If the answer is "it's immaterial," the clinical story you're pitching is smaller than the pitch implies. If it's "we don't want to disclose it yet," an equity reporter will read that as the story.
- Can you name the pharmacy chain, and will they comment? Without that, the partnership angle is a single unverifiable sentence.
- Are you prepared for the independent-optometry story? It's the most reportable angle here and the least flattering. Pitching it means inviting a reporter to call people who will criticize you.
- Do you have an executive who will speak on record about the DTC thesis being wrong? That angle only works with a real quote; without it, the reporter writes it anyway and you have no voice in it.
- No current news signal was supplied with this update. Every "why now" above rests on your own announcement, which means you're competing for attention against whatever else broke that day rather than attaching to it.
