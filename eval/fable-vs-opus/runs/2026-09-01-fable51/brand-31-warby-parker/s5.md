**Warby Parker bets its future on the stores it was built to disrupt**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail or e-commerce trade reporter (think a DTC/retail-economics beat, not a lifestyle desk) covering the "digitally native brands go physical" reckoning. The hook is that Warby Parker — a company that made its name proving glasses didn't need a showroom — is now scaling past 300 physical locations and says its retail customers outspend its online-only ones. That's a direct challenge to the online-first thesis Warby itself popularized. Not for local business-openings reporters, healthcare press, or personal-finance/product-review outlets — none of them cover the DTC-economics argument.
- **Why now:** The 40-store expansion announcement is the fresh peg; the underlying thesis (retail beats online-only for this company) is the story.
- **Decay:** week — the announcement itself is a single news cycle, but the DTC-goes-physical thesis has legs for a feature written in the following days.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — names the prevailing belief ("DTC brands don't need stores") and uses Warby's own retail-spend claim as evidence against it. Distinct from the angles below because the protagonist is the *business model contradiction itself*, not a specific store format or partnership.
- **Proof it needs:** The actual multiplier or dollar figure behind "retail customers spend more than online-only customers"; comment from Warby on whether retail is now treated as the primary channel rather than a showroom for the website; any data on store-driven vs. online-driven revenue growth.
- **Facts it rests on:** 40 new stores taking the total past 300; Warby says retail customers spend more than online-only customers.

Not for general retail-openings wire coverage, financial/markets reporters, or lifestyle press — this is specifically a health-services-in-retail story.
- **Why now:** The nationwide rollout of optometrist-staffed stores is the concrete news event anchoring the category claim.
- **Decay:** month — category-level trend pieces about retail-as-healthcare hold longer than a single announcement cycle, even though this specific rollout is the news hook.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective + category lens — the protagonist is the *category* (retail health), not the company's financials or its competitive position. Distinct from the contrarian angle above, which is about business-model economics, not clinical service delivery.

**Warby Parker says it's profitable — but won't say how much comes from eye exams**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A markets or retail-finance reporter used to pressure-testing profitability claims from newly-profitable public companies. The fact that Warby highlights profitability and a major new revenue lever (in-store exams) in the same breath, while declining to break out exam revenue, is the kind of disclosure gap a financial trade reporter would flag. Not for lifestyle, healthcare-access, or general retail-openings press — this is specifically a "show your work" financial-scrutiny story.
- **Why now:** The profitability claim and the exam-revenue rollout are being announced together, making the non-disclosure notable right now rather than a background fact.
- **Decay:** 24hr — tied tightly to the specific claims in this announcement; a financial reporter would need to act inside the same news cycle before the moment passes.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data/skepticism lens — the story is the *gap* in the numbers, not the numbers themselves. Distinct from the other three because the protagonist is the disclosure itself, not the stores, the category, or the partnership.
- **Proof it needs:** Actual profitability figures (net income, margin, timeframe); confirmation from Warby on why exam revenue isn't broken out; any analyst or company comment on whether exams are material to the profitability turn.
- **Facts it rests on:** Warby recently reached profitability; exam-revenue contribution is not broken out.
