**Replit is trying to turn its tens of millions of users into app publishers overnight**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A reporter covering the AI-coding-tools trend at a technology business outlet or software-industry trade publication. The story isn't that Replit shipped a feature — it's that a platform with a huge, largely non-technical user base is betting its core product on people who've never written code. Not for education press (a narrower, sharper version of this story exists below), security trade press, or general consumer-gadget outlets looking for a feature review.
- **Why now:** The agent launches today; this is the direct read on what the launch signals about where "vibe coding" is headed.
- **Decay:** 24hr — this is the trend angle riding directly on the launch itself, standard decay for company-news pegged to a product announcement.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder-of-abstraction lens, zoomed up — the category/market is the protagonist here, not any single user, founder, or investor.
- **Proof it needs:** independent context for "tens of millions of users" (growth trend, comparison point, or breakdown of engineer vs. non-engineer users) beyond the company's own framing; any real examples of apps built with the tool, even unnamed, to show usage beyond marketing language.
- **Facts it rests on:** Replit is launching an AI agent that builds and deploys full applications from a plain-English description; the agent writes code, sets up a database, and deploys a working app without the user writing code; Replit says it has tens of millions of users; it positions the agent for non-engineers and "vibe coders."

**Replit says anyone can build an app with plain English. It won't say how often those apps actually work.**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A tech-accountability or AI-skeptic reporter — someone who writes analysis testing vendor claims against reality, at a tech business outlet or publication known for that kind of scrutiny. The pitch to non-engineers is "you don't need to code." The one number that would back that up — the rate at which generated apps work without manual fixes — is the number Replit isn't sharing, and the audience being sold on it is the audience least equipped to catch a failure. Not for product-review or how-to outlets that would just cover this as a feature launch, education press, or enterprise security press.
- **Why now:** The gap between the pitch and the undisclosed number is most newsworthy at the moment the pitch is being made publicly.
- **Decay:** week — this needs a company response or independent testing to report properly, so it has more runway than the launch-day cycle but is still tied to this announcement.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — the protagonist is the marketed non-engineer end user, and the story is the mismatch between the "no code needed" framing and the withheld reliability data. Different lens and different stakes than the market-trend angle above.
- **Proof it needs:** Replit's actual success or error rate for agent-generated apps, or an on-record refusal to provide one; concrete examples of generated apps that needed no fixes versus ones that broke; a company comment on why the metric isn't published.
- **Facts it rests on:** the agent writes code, sets up a database, and deploys a working app without the user writing code; it positions the agent for non-engineers and "vibe coders"; reliability and how often generated apps work without fixes are not disclosed; Replit is privately held.

**Replit already lives in classrooms. Now it can write the homework too.**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education-technology or higher-ed reporter covering classroom tools, academic integrity, and how computer science gets taught. Replit's existing footprint in education collides directly with a tool that writes and deploys code from a plain-English prompt without the student touching it — that's a live curriculum and assessment question the moment it ships, not a hypothetical one. Not for security trade press, general consumer tech press, or enterprise/business reporters.
- **Why now:** The agent ships into a platform that's already embedded in classrooms, so the collision is immediate, not speculative.
- **Decay:** week — needs a teacher, professor, or district source to report properly; not same-day breaking news but stays live through the news cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective-shift lens, narrowed to the education segment specifically — a different protagonist and different stakes (learning outcomes, academic integrity) than the broad market-trend angle or the individual end-user framing of the reliability angle.
- **Proof it needs:** a named teacher, professor, or school on the record about classroom use of Replit and reaction to the new agent; any school or district guidance on AI-assisted coding assignments; a breakdown of how much of Replit's stated user base is students (not currently supplied).
- **Facts it rests on:** Replit is a browser-based coding platform used heavily in education; the agent writes code and deploys a working app without the user writing code; it positions the agent for non-engineers and "vibe coders."

**Replit's new agent lets non-coders spin up live databases. Who's checking what's in them?**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A cybersecurity or enterprise-IT trade reporter covering shadow IT, AppSec, and unmanaged software sprawl. The agent provisions live databases for people who, by the company's own positioning, aren't engineers — a larger on-ramp for unreviewed data infrastructure showing up inside organizations and schools, at the exact moment the company won't say how often the output is even correct. Not for education press, general consumer tech blogs, or outlets looking for a straightforward launch write-up.
- **Why now:** The risk is created at the moment the product ships and starts provisioning real infrastructure, not later.
- **Decay:** week — a security-analysis piece that needs research or an on-record comment, not a same-day news value.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values relevance lens applied to a different, materially affected audience than the reliability angle — here the risk lands on whoever's data ends up in a database a non-technical user configured, not on the builder's own experience.
- **Proof it needs:** documented examples of security misconfigurations in agent-generated apps (exposed credentials, open databases, missing auth); an on-record company description of what guardrails exist in the database-setup step; input from a security researcher who has tested the tool.
- **Facts it rests on:** the agent writes code, sets up a database, and deploys a working app without the user writing code; it positions the agent for non-engineers and "vibe coders"; reliability and how often generated apps work without fixes are not disclosed.

**Refused angles**

- "Replit's platform reaches tens of millions of users with its new AI agent" — `slop`. A bare, uncontextualized user count reads as a press-release stat, not a story.
- "Meet the founders betting Replit's future on an AI agent that codes for you" — `hallucinated_fact`. No founder names, backgrounds, or quotes were supplied.
- "One non-engineer built a real business overnight with Replit's new agent" — `hallucinated_fact`. No named customer or specific example exists in the facts.
- "Replit's usage-based pricing shows where AI agent costs are headed" — `duplicate`. Same protagonist and lens as the market-trend angle; the pricing detail is one unelaborated fact with no separate hook.
- "Privately held Replit doesn't have to answer to public markets on its AI bet" — `no_journalist_shape`. Being privately held is a status, not news, absent other facts to hang it on.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Has Replit shared, even privately, any number on how often agent-generated apps work without manual fixes? Without it, the reliability angle is a strong question but not yet a documented finding.
- What guardrails, if any, does the agent apply when it sets up a database for someone with no technical background — default auth, credential handling, access controls? Without an answer, the security angle is analysis, not a confirmed pattern.
- Is "used heavily in education" backed by any number (share of users who are students, number of schools), or is that a general characterization? That determines how hard the education angle can be pushed.
- What does "usage-based pricing on top of subscriptions" actually cost a typical user? Without a number, that detail can't support a standalone story.

**Next step:** `find-journalists` — once you pick one of these angles. The reliability and security angles are the sharpest but both need Replit's on-record comment or independent testing before they're pitch-ready; close that gap first, or be upfront that you're pitching a question, not a finding.
