**Replit's new agent ships a whole working app from one English sentence — no code from the user**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A developer-tools reporter at a technical trade outlet or a coding-focused newsletter that covers the "natural-language-to-software" shift. The hook is not "AI writes code" — many tools do that — it's that this agent claims to span the full chain a human developer would otherwise own: writes the code, stands up a database, and deploys a running app, with the user never touching code. That's the line worth testing: is this an end-to-end app builder or autocomplete with a marketing budget? Not for consumer-gadget desks, general business press, or AI-ethics columnists.
- **Why now:** The agent is launching now; the "describe an app and get a deployed app" claim is the news event.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard product-launch window. The broader natural-language-software thesis can extend to a week if a reporter wants to zoom out, but the launch peg is a one-day event.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — it treats the *capability* (full app from plain English, deploy included) as the story, not the company. The other angles drop down to the education buyer and over to the reliability question.
- **Proof it needs:** a live demo a reporter can run themselves, end to end; at least one fully described example app (what was prompted, what got built, what the database and deploy step actually produced); how this differs from code-generation tools that stop at a snippet.
- **Facts it rests on:** the agent writes code, sets up a database, and deploys a working app without the user writing code; Replit is a browser-based coding platform; it positions the agent for non-engineers and "vibe coders."

**Can a student who can't code now ship a real app? Replit is betting class on it**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education-technology reporter at an ed-tech trade outlet or a higher-ed/K-12 publication covering how AI changes what gets taught in computer-science and intro-tech classes. Replit is described as used heavily in education, and the agent is aimed squarely at people who can't code — which is most students before a course, not after. The honest tension a reporter can chase: does an agent that writes the code for you teach programming, or replace the reason to learn it? Not for enterprise dev-tools desks or VC reporters.
- **Why now:** The launch is the peg, but the education angle is only timely if Replit can point to actual classroom use of the agent, not just classroom use of the platform.
- **Decay:** week — ed-tech "what does this do to how we teach" stories run on a slower clock than the launch-day product story.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, customer protagonist (the education buyer / the student) — the story is what happens inside a classroom, not the capability or the funding. It's the only angle that uses the education fact as the spine.
- **Proof it needs:** a named school, course, or instructor using the agent (not just the platform) and willing to describe the result; whether educators see it as a teaching aid or a shortcut around learning; any classroom data on what students produced. Customer-story angles need at least one real, nameable user — this one currently has none.
- **Facts it rests on:** Replit is a browser-based coding platform used heavily in education; the agent is positioned for non-engineers; tens of millions of users.

**Replit will tell you it builds working apps — it won't tell you how often they actually work**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A developer-tools reporter or a skeptical-tech writer covering the gap between AI-coding marketing and what the tools ship in practice. The prevailing belief Replit is selling into: AI agents can now reliably build deployable software for non-engineers. The evidence cutting against the clean version of that story is in Replit's own disclosure — reliability and how often generated apps work without fixes are explicitly not disclosed. A reporter can make that omission the story: a product sold to people who by definition can't debug it, with no published success rate. Not for launch-coverage desks that just want the feature list, and not a hit piece — it's a fair-question piece.
- **Why now:** The omission is live as of the launch; the question lands hardest while the launch claims are fresh.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the contrarian read is strongest against the launch-day messaging; it goes stale as the news cycle moves unless Replit later releases numbers.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — it's built on a real, widely-pushed belief (AI builds working apps now) *and* a concrete piece of counter-evidence (no disclosed reliability). The first angle reports the claim; this one interrogates it. Different beat posture, different protagonist (the skeptic / the non-engineer who can't fix a broken app).
- **Proof it needs:** Replit's own answer on whether it tracks how often generated apps work without manual fixes, and whether it will publish a number; independent hands-on testing of several generated apps; what happens to a non-engineer when a deployed app breaks. Contrarian angles require evidence against the belief — here the evidence is the non-disclosure itself, which a reporter must confirm directly with the company.
- **Facts it rests on:** reliability and how often generated apps work without fixes are not disclosed; the agent is positioned for non-engineers and "vibe coders"; the agent deploys a working app without the user writing code.

**You pay per build: Replit's agent charges usage on top of your subscription**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A SaaS / software-business reporter at a trade outlet covering how AI features get priced — specifically the shift from flat subscriptions to metered, usage-based charging on top of a subscription. The hook is the pricing model, not the agent: a tool aimed at non-engineers and students (cost-sensitive buyers) that bills usage-based on top of an existing subscription is a live example of the "AI compute gets passed to the user" pattern. Not for product reviewers or education reporters — this is a pricing-and-business-model beat.
- **Why now:** The pricing model ships with the launch; it's a concrete data point for the broader metered-AI-pricing story reporters are tracking.
- **Decay:** week — pricing-trend pieces run slower than launch news and can pull in this example after launch day.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the-money protagonist applied to pricing structure rather than capability — the story is *how it's billed*, which no other kept angle touches. Distinct beat (software business model) from the dev-tools and ed-tech angles.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual usage-based rates and what a "unit" is (per build? per deploy? per token?); a worked example of what a typical student or non-engineer project would cost on top of the subscription; how this compares to flat-fee coding tools. Without real numbers this is a topic, not a story.
- **Facts it rests on:** pricing is usage-based on top of subscriptions; the agent is positioned for non-engineers and "vibe coders"; Replit is used heavily in education.

**Refused angles**

- "Replit revolutionizes software development with its game-changing AI agent" — `slop`.
- "Replit's tens of millions of users prove vibe coding is the future" — `hallucinated_fact`. The user count is given, but "proves vibe coding is the future" is an unsupported leap; the facts don't say how many of those users will adopt the agent or what they'll build.
- "Replit profile: the company taking on traditional software engineering" — `no_journalist_shape`. No founder facts, no named operator, no funding event, and no current peg were supplied; there is nothing to build a profile or counterposition around.
- "Why this launch threatens incumbent dev platforms" — `hallucinated_fact`. No competitor, no incumbent response, and no market-share fact were provided; naming a threatened rival would be invention.
- "The non-engineer angle on Replit's launch" — `duplicate`. Collapses into the education customer-story and the dev-tools category angles; "non-engineers" is the audience for both, not a separate story.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Will Replit publish *any* reliability number — success rate, fix-free rate, anything? If not, the launch claim "deploys a working app" rests on the company's word alone, and the contrarian angle is the strongest story in this set.
- Is the agent actually being used in classrooms, or is "used heavily in education" a fact about the platform, not the agent? The whole education angle depends on the answer, and you have no named school.
- Can a reporter run the full describe-to-deployed-app flow live, with no human intervention, on a non-trivial prompt? A canned demo won't survive a dev-tools reporter, and most of these angles need a real one.
- What does usage-based pricing actually cost a student or hobbyist for a typical project? Without the numbers, the pricing angle is unpitchable and the education angle has a hidden cost story attached to it.
- "Tens of millions of users" is a platform number — how many have access to or have used the new agent? Pitching the user count as agent traction would be a fact you don't have.
