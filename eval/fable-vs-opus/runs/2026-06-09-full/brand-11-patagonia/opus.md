**Patagonia opens free repairs at every US store and adds mail-in for the whole country**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A sustainable-fashion or circular-economy trade reporter covering repair, resale, and "right to repair" in apparel. The hook is the operational reach — repair as a standing in-store service at 70+ locations plus a national mail-in channel, not a pop-up or a marketing stunt. This reporter can test whether free repair-at-scale is becoming a category norm or stays a Patagonia outlier. Not for general consumer-deals press, fast-fashion trend desks, or anyone who'll treat "free repairs" as a coupon story.
- **Why now:** The nationwide rollout is the live event today — both the all-store availability and the mail-in launch are new as of now.
- **Decay:** 24hr — this is a launch/expansion announcement; the rollout itself is a one-day news event unless tied to a broader repair-economy trend, which extends it to about a week.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens (relevance + magnitude) at the operational altitude — the story is the infrastructure of repair-at-scale, not the founder or the resale number. It differs from the data angle below, which is about Worn Wear resale, and from the contrarian angle, which is about what the company won't disclose.
- **Proof it needs:** the exact store count and which markets the mail-in channel covers; what "basic" vs "complex" means in practice (turnaround time, typical at-cost charge); any repair-volume figure the company can share. Patagonia did not provide repair counts or capacity, so those holes must be filled before pitching.
- **Facts it rests on:** Worn Wear repair is now available in all US stores; mail-in repairs are launching nationwide; customers can drop off any Patagonia item at 70+ US stores or mail it in; basic repairs are free, complex ones charged at cost.

- **Why now:** The figure is being surfaced alongside today's repair expansion, giving it a peg; on its own the cumulative total is not time-pressured.
- **Decay:** week — a data/context piece can run after the launch day if the number is properly framed.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens (scale) with a different protagonist — the resale platform and its cumulative output, not the in-store repair service. The 500,000 figure is the engine here; repair is the engine in the angle above.
- **Proof it needs:** the time window the 500,000 covers and whether it's accelerating or flat; the share of revenue resale represents — which Patagonia explicitly will not disclose, so a scale-and-growth story has to be built without it, or the gap named openly; comparable branded-resale figures the user would have to source themselves.
- **Facts it rests on:** Worn Wear has now resold over 500,000 used items; the company will not say what share of revenue resale represents.

**The retailer that won't say how much money its sustainability story makes**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A business or retail reporter skeptical of corporate sustainability claims, covering the gap between green positioning and disclosed economics. The prevailing belief: brands tout circular programs to look good while the real business runs elsewhere; the evidence in tension with it is that Patagonia is expanding repair and resale meaningfully yet refuses to disclose what share of revenue resale represents. That refusal is the story — is repair-and-resale a genuine business line or a brand halo the company won't put numbers behind? Not for anyone covering Patagonia as an uncritical sustainability hero.
- **Why now:** The refusal is fresh and concrete — it sits directly against today's high-visibility expansion, which is exactly when a skeptical reporter can ask it.
- **Decay:** week — a scrutiny/analysis piece keeps for several days while the launch is in the air.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion/contrarian lens — it cuts against the "Patagonia as model" narrative using the company's own non-disclosure as the evidence. The other angles take the expansion at face value; this one interrogates it. It needs a real, widely-held belief (green-marketing skepticism) and real evidence against the easy read (the explicit refusal to disclose) — both are present.
- **Proof it needs:** the exact wording and scope of what Patagonia declines to disclose; whether the company breaks out any repair/resale economics anywhere (it being privately held, there may be no public financials at all); an analyst or comparable brand willing to comment on resale margins. Do not assert the program is unprofitable or a halo — the only fact is the non-disclosure.
- **Facts it rests on:** the company will not say what share of revenue resale represents; Patagonia is privately held and directs profits to environmental causes; the repair and resale programs are being expanded as described.
