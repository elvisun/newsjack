**The company everyone wrote off just posted its first profitable quarter in three years**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-business or markets reporter covering subscription hardware companies and post-pandemic demand corrections. The prevailing belief is that pandemic-era connected-fitness winners are in permanent decline; positive adjusted EBITDA and free cash flow after three years of losses is real evidence against it. Not for fitness-lifestyle press, product reviewers, or general startup blogs — this is an earnings-and-turnaround story.
- **Why now:** The quarter was just reported. Earnings are a same-day hook; after the first wave of wire coverage, the angle only survives if you offer something the press release didn't contain.
- **Decay:** 4hr — earnings are a same-day market signal; the contrarian frame goes stale once the result is priced in and written up.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — names the widely-held "Peloton is dead" belief and counters it with the supplied result. The other kept angles are about the rental model, the retention data, and the CEO, not the verdict on the company.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual adjusted EBITDA and free cash flow figures; an executive available same-day for comment beyond the release; an honest, on-record answer to the net-loss and debt question, because any reporter taking this angle will ask it in the first call.
- **Facts it rests on:** first profitable quarter in three years; positive adjusted EBITDA and free cash flow; the company remains unprofitable on a net basis and carries significant debt.

**Peloton's nationwide rental push tests whether people want to subscribe to exercise equipment, not own it**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A subscription-economy or consumer-spending reporter at a business outlet covering the ownership-to-access shift — the beat that tracks car subscriptions, furniture rental, and hardware-as-a-service. A national rollout of a bike-plus-membership bundle with no commitment is a concrete data point for that trend, from a company with 6 million members. Not for earnings-focused markets desks or fitness-gear reviewers.
- **Why now:** The nationwide expansion is the hook; the trend frame gives it a longer runway than the earnings day.
- **Decay:** week — trend pieces about the rental model can run after the earnings cycle, but the "expanding nationwide" peg fades within days.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — the protagonist is the access-over-ownership category, not Peloton's quarter. Structurally different from the earnings verdict and the retention data story.
- **Proof it needs:** the monthly rental price; rental-cohort numbers (sign-ups, conversion to purchase, churn versus owners); when the nationwide expansion actually starts; an executive who can speak to the unit economics of renting out hardware.
- **Facts it rests on:** the rental program bundles a bike and membership for one monthly fee with no commitment; it is expanding nationwide; total members exceed 6 million.

**Record-low churn is doing more for Peloton than any new bike**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A data-minded reporter covering subscription-business metrics — the kind who writes about retention curves, LTV, and why churn is the number that decides subscription companies' fates. Record-low churn alongside a swing to positive cash flow is a clean cause-and-effect candidate that beat can interrogate. Not for general consumer press or anyone who won't print a churn rate.
- **Why now:** The churn figure ties to the just-reported quarter; retention analysis pieces can run through the week after earnings.
- **Decay:** week — data and context angles outlive the earnings-day cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, change-over-time — the protagonist is a single metric and what it explains. The trend angle is about the rental category; this is about the retention number itself.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual churn rate and the prior record it beats; enough historical churn data to show the trajectory; an explanation of what drove it that survives scrutiny (the supplied facts don't say why churn fell — do not let a pitch imply the rental program caused it without evidence).
- **Facts it rests on:** subscriber churn fell to a record low; positive adjusted EBITDA and free cash flow for the quarter; total members exceed 6 million.

**Inside the $200M cost-cutting playbook that got Peloton to its first profitable quarter in three years**

- **Story type:** exec-spotlight
- **Why a journalist cares:** A management or corporate-strategy reporter at a business outlet covering turnarounds — the beat that profiles operators who fix broken companies rather than found new ones. Over $200M in annual cost cuts under a new CEO, landing in a profitable quarter, is the concrete spine such a profile needs. Not for fitness trade press or markets reporters who only want the numbers.
- **Why now:** The profitable quarter is the proof point that makes the turnaround profile timely; without it this is just a cost-cutting story.
- **Decay:** week — the profile rides the earnings news but can be reported and placed in the days after it.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, CEO protagonist — the person and the decisions are the story, not the result. The contrarian angle delivers the verdict; this one explains how it happened.
- **Proof it needs:** the CEO's name and willingness to sit for an interview (the supplied facts don't name them); a breakdown of where the $200M came from — headcount, logistics, retail, marketing; at least one hard decision the CEO can describe on record.
- **Facts it rests on:** Peloton cut over $200M in annual costs under a new CEO; first profitable quarter in three years; the company remains unprofitable on a net basis and carries significant debt.

**Refused angles**

- "Peloton is back: the great comeback story" — `slop`. Comeback puffery with no specific protagonist or beat; the contrarian angle covers the honest version with the caveats attached.
- "Peloton's rental program is revolutionizing fitness" — `slop`. Undefended superlative; the trend angle is the defensible version.
- "Six million members proves connected fitness won" — `no_journalist_shape`. A bare membership number with no context, comparison, or trend attached is a topic, not a story; no specific beat would run it.
- "How one renter got fit without buying a bike" — `hallucinated_fact`. No named customer or renter was supplied; a customer-story angle cannot exist until one does.
- "Peloton's results signal a fitness-industry recovery" — `hallucinated_fact`. The supplied facts cover one company; there is no industry data here to support a market-wide claim.
- A breaking newsjack angle tied to a current external story — `no_why_now_but_required`. No detector signal was supplied; nothing here justifies a 30min hook.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Peloton is a public company and the quarter has been reported — the headline result is already wire coverage. What do you have that wasn't in the release: the churn number, rental cohort data, or CEO access? Without at least one, the first angle is a rephrasing of news reporters already wrote.
- What is the on-record answer when a reporter asks whether adjusted-EBITDA profitability is window dressing over a net loss and significant debt? Every angle above gets this question; if there's no good answer, the contrarian angle in particular will turn on you.
- What actually drove churn to a record low? "It fell" is a fact; "why it fell" is the story, and the facts supplied don't say.
- What does the rental program cost per month, and does renting out depreciating hardware make money? A subscription-economy reporter will ask before writing a word.
- Is the new CEO named, media-trained, and available this week? The exec-spotlight angle is dead without the person.
