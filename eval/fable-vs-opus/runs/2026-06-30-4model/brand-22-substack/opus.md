**Substack adds native video as it pushes from newsletter tool to media app**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A creator-economy or digital-media reporter at a media-business trade outlet or media newsletter that covers platform strategy (the YouTube/Patreon/Substack competitive set). The hook isn't "video shipped" — it's that a company that built its identity on writing is now letting creators record, host, and monetize video and podcasts directly, alongside a TikTok-style feed. That is a positioning shift from publishing tool to media app, and this beat can test whether the bet holds. Not for general tech-gadget press, consumer app reviewers, or pure VC-funding desks.
- **Why now:** The native video launch is live now; the "media app" repositioning is the timely frame the launch makes concrete.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the launch itself is a one-day news event; the repositioning thesis can stretch a few days for a reporter who wants the strategy angle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — this makes the company's *strategic direction* (publishing tool → media app) the story, not the feature. The other angles drop to the money and over to the creator-class question.
- **Proof it needs:** what the native video tooling actually does that creators couldn't do before by embedding third-party video; whether the TikTok-style feed surfaces video differently from text; a Substack product or exec source willing to articulate the media-app strategy on record. Any claim about competitors must come with a source — the facts here name no competitor.
- **Facts it rests on:** writers can record, host, and monetize video and podcasts directly; the company has leaned into a "media app" strategy with a TikTok-style feed.

**Substack says its top creators made over $400M last year — but won't say who or how much each**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A creator-economy data/business reporter at a media-business outlet who covers platform payout claims and is trained to be skeptical of aggregate "creators earned $X" numbers. The hook is the gap: a big collective figure ($400M+ from "top creators") with no individual breakdown and no definition of "top." That gap is the story for a reporter who pressure-tests platform economics. Not for outlets that will simply reprint the $400M figure as a win.
- **Why now:** The figure is being asserted now alongside the video launch, which is the moment to interrogate it.
- **Decay:** week — a numbers-scrutiny piece doesn't have to run on launch day; it holds while the claim circulates.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens (scale, plus the contrarian inversion of "no disclosure where disclosure was expected") — the angle is the *unverifiability* of the number, not the number as a triumph. Different protagonist (the metric and its holes) from the strategy and creator-class angles.
- **Proof it needs:** Substack's definition of "top creators" and the cohort size; whether $400M is gross to creators or after the 10% cut; how this compares to prior years (the facts give no trend); ideally an independent or named-creator data point. Without these, the number stays a claim, not a finding.
- **Facts it rests on:** Substack says its top creators collectively earned over $400M last year; individual creator earnings beyond the aggregate are not disclosed; the platform takes a 10% cut of subscription revenue.

**As Substack chases video, the 10% cut becomes the question creators have to answer**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A creator-economy reporter covering the working economics of full-time creators and platform take rates. With native video, Substack is asking creators to host more of their work — and their revenue — on a platform that takes 10% of subscriptions, the same cut now extending over video and podcast monetization. The beat that cares is the one that covers creator livelihoods and platform lock-in, not platform features. Not for funding/markets desks or product reviewers.
- **Why now:** The video launch is the moment the take-rate question gets bigger, because more creator activity (and money) now runs through Substack.
- **Decay:** week — a creator-economics piece holds past launch day.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, creator/customer protagonist — the story is what the change means for the creator's economics, distinct from the company-strategy angle (the platform's protagonist) and the metric-scrutiny angle (the number's protagonist).
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named creator who will speak to whether 10% on video/podcast revenue changes the math for them; how Substack's 10% compares to where else creators host video (the facts name no comparison); whether the cut applies identically to video and podcast monetization.
- **Facts it rests on:** writers can record, host, and monetize video and podcasts directly; the platform takes a 10% cut of subscription revenue; over 4 million paid subscriptions across all publications.

**Substack is spending on a media-app build while privately held and short on funding**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A media-business or startup-finance reporter covering how privately held platforms fund expensive feature bets. The tension is the hook: a company with publicly reported funding struggles is investing in native video hosting and a TikTok-style feed — capital-intensive infrastructure — without disclosed new funding here. The beat that cares covers whether the company can afford its own strategy. Not for creator-tools coverage or product press.
- **Why now:** The video launch is the spend; the funding question is what a finance reporter attaches to it now.
- **Decay:** 24hr — tied to the launch as the news peg; the underlying funding question is longer-lived but only timely because of today's announcement.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the-money protagonist — this is about Substack's own balance sheet and runway, not creator economics or the product story. Note: the only funding fact here is "public funding struggles," which is thin; this angle is largely a question, not a finding.
- **Proof it needs:** what the "public funding struggles" actually refer to (the facts only assert them); whether video hosting was built in-house or via a partner; any disclosed funding, runway, or revenue figure beyond the 10% cut and 4M subscriptions. Without these, this is a line of questioning, not a story.
- **Facts it rests on:** Substack is privately held and has had public funding struggles; the company has leaned into a "media app" strategy with a TikTok-style feed; writers can record, host, and monetize video directly.

**Refused angles**

- "Substack revolutionizes the creator economy with native video" — `slop`.
- "Substack takes on YouTube and TikTok with video launch" — `hallucinated_fact`. The "TikTok-style feed" is in the facts, but no competitor is named and no head-to-head claim is supported; naming rivals as combatants invents the conflict.
- "4 million paid subscriptions prove Substack has won the newsletter wars" — `hallucinated_fact`. 4M paid subscriptions is a fact; "won the wars" is an undefended superlative with no comparison data.
- "What Substack's video launch means for the future of media" — `no_journalist_shape`. No specific sub-beat, no protagonist, no why-now beyond a vibe.
- "The podcast angle on Substack's new tools" — `duplicate`. Same launch fact, same media-app lens as the lead angle; podcasts are bundled into the same feature, not a structurally separate story.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Does $400M+ count gross creator revenue or revenue after Substack's 10% cut, and how many creators count as "top"? Until you can answer both, the headline number is a claim a good reporter will pick apart in the first reply.
- Will any individual creator go on record with their own earnings or their video plans? Every creator-facing angle here gets dramatically stronger with one named voice and dramatically weaker without it.
- What exactly does native video do that creators couldn't already do by embedding video, and is the TikTok-style feed new or already shipped? The launch's news value depends on the answer.
- What are the "public funding struggles," concretely? You're resting a whole funding angle on an asserted fact with no detail, dates, or figures behind it.
- The facts name no competitor. Any angle that pits Substack against YouTube, Patreon, or TikTok is something you'd be importing, not something these facts support — do you have a source for it?
