**Skydio aims its pilotless inspection drone at utilities caught between Chinese hardware and import restrictions**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A drone-policy or supply-chain-security reporter at a defense-tech or policy trade outlet covering the squeeze on Chinese drone makers. The hook is the substitution question: when import restrictions bite, is there a US-made drone that actually does the job, or just a flag on a spec sheet? Skydio's launch is a concrete test case. Not for utility-operations trade press, consumer gadget reviewers, or general startup desks.
- **Why now:** The product launch is live today, and the company is explicitly positioning against Chinese drone makers facing import restrictions — a fight this beat is already covering.
- **Decay:** week — the launch peg is a 24-hour event, but the US-versus-Chinese-drone substitution question supports a longer analysis window.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, competitor/incumbent protagonist — the story is the category's power balance under trade policy, not the product's features. The other angles stay inside the utility, the company strategy, and the inspection workforce.
- **Proof it needs:** specifics on which import restrictions the company means and whom they hit; substantiation of the "US-made" claim (where it's built, supply chain); an on-record executive willing to make the China comparison directly rather than by implication.
- **Facts it rests on:** Skydio is privately held and US-made, positioning against Chinese drone makers facing import restrictions; the company is launching an autonomous inspection drone for utility and infrastructure routes.

**Skydio's new drone docks at the substation and files its own defect reports**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A transmission-and-distribution or utility-operations trade reporter covering inspection technology and grid maintenance. The dock-charge-schedule loop with automatic defect reports is the operational story: inspection as a standing service rather than a crewed event. This reporter can test whether "no pilot" survives contact with real utility workflows. Not for policy desks, business-of-drones reporters, or general tech press.
- **Why now:** The launch is today, and the company says it has contracts with several US utilities — though none is named, which is the angle's open wound.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard product-launch cycle for trade press; without a named utility it has no second life.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed down to the concrete workflow — the substation, the dock, the scheduled flight, the report. The geopolitics and strategy angles sit several rungs up.
- **Proof it needs:** one named utility, or at minimum one willing to speak on background about deployment; defect-detection accuracy data or an example report; flight cadence, dock uptime, and what the FAA has authorized for scheduled flights with no pilot on site.
- **Facts it rests on:** the drone docks, charges, and runs scheduled inspection flights of power lines and substations, sending defect reports automatically; the company says it has contracts with several US utilities.

**Two years after killing its consumer drones, Skydio shows what it bet the company on**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A commercial-drone-industry trade reporter or a business reporter who covered the consumer-drone shakeout. This is the follow-up chapter: a company that publicly walked away from consumers now shipping the product that was supposed to justify it. The beat already told the exit story; this is the verdict installment. Not for utility trade press or policy reporters.
- **Why now:** The launch is the first hard evidence of whether the two-year-old pivot produced a real enterprise product, not just a strategy memo.
- **Decay:** week — follow-up strategy pieces can run after launch day, but the peg fades once the launch news is absorbed.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, follow-up — the protagonist is the company's bet over time, not the product, the trade war, or the customer. It's the only angle where the consumer-market exit is the spine rather than background.
- **Proof it needs:** an executive on record about what the consumer exit cost and what it freed up; any disclosed revenue mix or enterprise customer-count trajectory since the exit; the timeline of the pivot in the company's own numbers, not adjectives.
- **Facts it rests on:** Skydio exited the consumer-drone market two years ago to focus on enterprise and public-sector customers; the company is now launching an autonomous inspection drone and says it has contracts with several US utilities.

**A drone with no pilot is a shot at the inspection business built on pilots**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A commercial-drone-services trade reporter covering the drone-pilot economy and inspection contractors. "No pilot" is the conflict: scheduled autonomous flights replace exactly the crewed inspection work an entire services layer sells to utilities. This reporter can ask who loses the contract when the dock wins it. Not for general business desks, policy reporters, or consumer press.
- **Why now:** The launch makes the threat concrete today; the displacement question has a longer shelf life than the announcement.
- **Decay:** week — the labor-and-services analysis outlives the launch-day cycle but needs the launch as its entry point.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, conflict, with the incumbent services workforce as protagonist — structurally different from the other counterposition angle, where the threatened party is Chinese manufacturers, not human pilots.
- **Proof it needs:** the FAA authorization status that lets scheduled flights run with no pilot — without it the whole premise wobbles; a cost or frequency comparison versus piloted inspection that the company will defend on record; what happens to the pilot or contractor roles at the utilities under contract.
- **Facts it rests on:** the drone flies utility and infrastructure routes with no pilot and runs scheduled inspection flights; the company says it has contracts with several US utilities.

**Refused angles**

- "Skydio revolutionizes infrastructure inspection with cutting-edge autonomy" — `slop`.
- "Utility customer slashes inspection costs with Skydio drones" — `hallucinated_fact`: no named utility customer is disclosed, and no cost or outcome data was supplied. This is the customer-story everyone wants; it cannot be written yet.
- "Skydio proves American drone manufacturing can beat China" — `hallucinated_fact`: one product launch is not market-level evidence; the defensible version of this is the counterposition angle above, which claims only what the facts support.
- "Pitch this against the latest grid-failure or wildfire headlines" — `no_why_now_but_required`: no current news signal was supplied, and inventing one is the forced-relevance failure this skill refuses.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Can any of the "several US utilities" be named, or speak even on background? Every angle here is materially weaker without one, and trade reporters will ask in the first reply.
- What exactly has the FAA authorized for scheduled flights with no pilot? If the answer is a waiver still pending, "no pilot" is a regulatory story you don't control — and a reporter may write that one instead.
- How many utilities is "several," and are these paid deployments or pilots? That word will not survive a fact-check follow-up.
- "Sending defect reports automatically" — what is the detection accuracy, and who verifies the reports today? A claim of automation with no error rate attached invites the skeptical version of the story.
