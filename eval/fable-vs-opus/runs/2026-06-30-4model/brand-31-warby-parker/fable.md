**The poster child of online-only retail now runs more than 300 stores**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail or e-commerce reporter at a business outlet who covers DTC economics — the writers who spent a decade on the "digital-native brands don't need stores" thesis and its unwinding. Warby Parker was the canonical example of that thesis, and this expansion is hard evidence against it: 40 new stores, a count past 300, profitability arriving alongside the retail push, and the company's own claim that retail customers spend more than online-only ones. Not for local business desks covering individual store openings, optometry trade press, or consumer shopping/style writers.
- **Why now:** The 40-store expansion announcement is the live event; the contrarian frame only works while the announcement is the news.
- **Decay:** week — the announcement itself is a 24-hour event, but the DTC-reversal trend frame can support a reported piece for about a week after.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — a real prevailing belief (DTC means online-first, stores are the cost center) against real supplied evidence (300+ stores, profitability, higher retail spend). The other kept angles are about healthcare, investors, and distribution; this one is about the death of a retail thesis.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual figure behind "retail customers spend more than online-only customers" — a multiple or dollar comparison, not the bare claim; confirmation of how much of the path to profitability the company attributes to retail; store-count history to show the trajectory.
- **Facts it rests on:** 40 new stores; store count past 300; recently reached profitability; company says retail customers spend more than online-only customers.

**Warby Parker's next act is eye exams, not frames**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A healthcare-business or retail-health reporter — the beat that covers retailers becoming care providers (pharmacy clinics, retail primary care). Most of the 40 new stores will have optometrist-staffed comprehensive exams, which moves Warby Parker from selling a product to delivering a regulated clinical service nationwide. That's a structural story about who provides routine eye care in the US, with a company-shaped example to hang it on. Not for general startup press, fashion/style desks, or markets reporters (they get the investor angle below).
- **Why now:** The nationwide exam expansion is announced now; the retail-health framing rides that announcement.
- **Decay:** week — the clinical-services frame is a trend story with a reported-piece window, not a same-day hit.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the category as protagonist — the story is "retail becomes healthcare," not "a brand opens stores." Different beat, different reader, different so-what from the DTC-reversal angle.
- **Proof it needs:** how many of the 40 stores will offer exams and the total exam-capable store count; whether optometrists are employees or affiliated practices (this changes the regulatory story state by state); exam pricing and whether insurance is accepted; anything the company will say about exam volume, since exam-revenue contribution is not broken out.
- **Facts it rests on:** expanding in-store comprehensive eye exams nationwide; most new stores will offer optometrist-staffed eye exams; store count past 300.

**Warby Parker got profitable — now it's spending on stores, and won't say what the exams earn**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** An equities or consumer-retail markets reporter covering publicly traded specialty retail. The tension is real and supplied: a newly profitable public company is funding a 40-store buildout on the claim that retail customers spend more, while declining to break out exam-revenue contribution. That's a unit-economics story a markets reporter can interrogate — does the spend-more claim survive scrutiny, and why isn't exam revenue disclosed? Not for trade optometry press, trend writers, or local press; this one needs someone who reads filings.
- **Why now:** The expansion announcement is the peg; markets coverage of a public company's capital-allocation decision runs on the announcement cycle.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard public-company news; after the cycle it needs an earnings event to revive.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, investor/money protagonist — the story is whether the expansion math works, not whether the trend is real. The undisclosed exam-revenue figure is the hook here and a liability everywhere else.
- **Proof it needs:** the quantified retail-vs-online spend claim with methodology; capex or investment figure for the 40 stores; an on-record answer to why exam revenue isn't broken out; profitability basis (GAAP vs. adjusted) before any pitch repeats "profitable."
- **Facts it rests on:** publicly traded; recently reached profitability; 40 new stores; company says retail customers spend more than online-only customers; exam-revenue contribution not broken out.

**The DTC brand that cut out middlemen is piloting sales through a pharmacy chain**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail-trade reporter covering pharmacy retail or wholesale distribution. Warby Parker's founding pitch was eliminating the middleman; a pilot to sell glasses through a national pharmacy chain is the brand adopting the channel it was built against, and it lands in the middle of pharmacy chains' fight to reinvent the front of store. Not for healthcare-policy reporters, founder-profile writers, or anyone covering the store expansion itself — this is a distribution story.
- **Why now:** The pilot is disclosed alongside the expansion news; the angle is pitchable while that disclosure is fresh, but only once the chain can be named.
- **Decay:** week — a pilot disclosure isn't a same-day event, and the channel-strategy frame holds for a reported piece.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the incumbent channel as protagonist — wholesale distribution through a third party is structurally different from owned stores, exams, or investor math. It's also the one angle where Warby is the guest in someone else's story.
- **Proof it needs:** the pharmacy chain's name — without it this angle cannot be pitched at all; pilot scope (store count, markets, what's stocked); whether exams are involved or it's frames-only.
- **Facts it rests on:** piloting a partnership to sell glasses through a national pharmacy chain.

**Refused angles**

- "Warby Parker revolutionizes how America gets eye care" — `slop`.
- "Warby Parker's expansion creates hundreds of optometrist jobs nationwide" — `hallucinated_fact`: no hiring numbers were supplied.
- A standalone data angle on "retail customers spend more than online-only customers" — `duplicate`: without a number it's a claim, not a dataset, and the claim already powers the markets angle.
- "What Warby Parker's growth means for the eyewear industry" — `no_journalist_shape`: no specific beat, no protagonist, a topic rather than a story.
- Any breaking-news peg tying this to a current retail or healthcare headline — `no_why_now_but_required`: no current news signal was supplied, and this skill won't fabricate one.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Exam-revenue contribution is not broken out. Will the company put any number on it — even exam volume or attach rate? Without something, the healthcare angle is a strategy claim, and a markets reporter will treat the silence itself as the story.
- "Retail customers spend more" — how much more, measured how, over what period? A bare comparative will not survive first contact with a reporter.
- Which pharmacy chain, and is the pilot confirmable on record? An unnamed partner kills the counterposition angle outright.
- Are the optometrists Warby Parker employees? Several states restrict corporate employment of optometrists; the answer changes the retail-health story materially.
- Is "recently reached profitability" GAAP profitability? Pitching the wrong version of that word to a markets reporter burns credibility.
