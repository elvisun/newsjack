**Whatnot's $3B year tests whether US shoppers will buy the way Asia already does**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** An e-commerce or retail-tech reporter at a business outlet or commerce trade publication who covers the US-versus-Asia live-commerce gap. Whatnot itself frames the bet — live commerce is far larger in Asia, and it's wagering the US catches up — and $3B in annual sales is the first US-scale data point big enough to test that thesis against. Not for consumer gadget reviewers, collectibles hobby press, or general startup blogs; this is a market-structure story, not a product story.
- **Why now:** The $3B milestone was just announced; it's the freshest US evidence either side of the "will live shopping work in America" debate has to argue with.
- **Decay:** week — the milestone announcement itself fades in a day, but the market-thesis framing supports a trend piece for about a week.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed all the way up — the protagonist is the US live-commerce market, with Whatnot as the evidence. The other kept angles drop down to the format, the sellers, and the money.
- **Proof it needs:** clarity on whether $3B is gross merchandise sales or net revenue, and the measurement period; any third-party or audited verification of the figure (it's currently self-reported); supplied comparison data on Asian live-commerce scale before any "X times larger in Asia" claim goes in a pitch.
- **Facts it rests on:** Whatnot says it crossed $3B in annual livestream-commerce sales; the company says live commerce is far larger in Asia and it is betting the US catches up; sellers run live video auctions and shoppers buy in real time.

**Whatnot grew to $3B on trading cards. Fashion and electronics will show whether live auctions are a niche or a channel**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A marketplace or retail trade reporter who covers how commerce formats move between categories — the kind who asks whether a mechanic that works for scarce, fandom-driven goods (trading cards, collectibles) transfers to commodity categories like fashion and electronics, where incumbent marketplaces already dominate. The tension is the story: collectibles reward live auctions (scarcity, hype, community); jeans and laptops may not. Not for founder-profile writers or local business desks, and not for collectibles hobby media, who already know the origin story.
- **Why now:** The expansion into fashion and electronics is announced alongside the $3B figure — the category bet is the live decision, not the milestone.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard company-news cycle for the expansion announcement; without early category data the story has no second week.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, category/incumbent protagonist — this is about whether the format survives outside its native habitat, not about market size (angle 1), seller livelihoods (angle 3), or unit economics (angle 4).
- **Proof it needs:** any data on fashion or electronics sales to date — share of GMV, seller count, or growth rate in the new categories; at least one seller who has moved from collectibles into a new category; an honest answer to what Whatnot offers in fashion/electronics that incumbent marketplaces don't.
- **Facts it rests on:** the platform started with trading cards and collectibles and is now adding new categories (fashion and electronics); sellers run live video auctions and shoppers buy in real time; Whatnot says it crossed $3B in annual sales.

**The livestream auctioneers behind Whatnot's $3 billion**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A creator-economy or future-of-work reporter at a business outlet or labor-and-platforms trade publication. The sellers are the protagonists: people running live video auctions as a business, on camera, in real time. $3B in platform sales implies real individual livelihoods — but the company hasn't disclosed how earnings are distributed, so the reporter's first question is whether this is a broad middle class of sellers or a few whales. Not for VC or markets reporters, and not for retail-tech desks covering the platform itself.
- **Why now:** The $3B milestone gives a seller-livelihood piece its peg; without it this is an evergreen platform-work story.
- **Decay:** week — seller-economy features can run after the announcement day if the human material is strong.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, seller protagonist, zoomed down the ladder to individual people — the only kept angle where the company is background rather than subject.
- **Proof it needs:** two or three named sellers willing to share real revenue numbers on record; the seller-earnings distribution (median earnings, not just top-seller anecdotes — currently undisclosed); the commission rate sellers actually pay. Without the distribution data, any "sellers are making a living" claim is unverifiable.
- **Facts it rests on:** sellers run live video auctions and shoppers buy in real time; Whatnot takes a commission on sales; $3B in annual livestream-commerce sales (company-reported); seller-earnings distribution is not fully disclosed.

**$3B in sales, a $5B valuation, and an undisclosed take-rate: the math under Whatnot's milestone**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A marketplace-economics or private-markets reporter who covers the gap between GMV headlines and actual revenue. The hook: a privately held company last valued near $5B announces $3B in sales, but the figure that converts sales into revenue — the commission take-rate — isn't fully disclosed. Whether the valuation looks cheap or rich depends entirely on that number. Not for consumer press, creator-economy writers, or trade reporters covering the category expansion. Fair warning: this angle only works if the company is willing to put real economics on record; pitched without them, it invites the skeptical version of the story.
- **Why now:** The milestone announcement is the peg; private-company economics stories run when the company hands reporters a new headline number.
- **Decay:** week — analysis of a milestone can trail the announcement by several days.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, money protagonist (the investor/markets view), plus the data lens applied to the one number disclosed and the one number withheld. Angles 1-3 never touch the valuation.
- **Proof it needs:** the take-rate, or at least a revenue figure; whether $3B is GMV or revenue and over what trailing period; the source and date of the "nearly $5B" valuation; a spokesperson prepared to discuss unit economics on record.
- **Facts it rests on:** Whatnot takes a commission on sales; the company is privately held and last valued at nearly $5B; it says it crossed $3B in annual livestream-commerce sales; take-rate and seller-earnings distribution not fully disclosed.

**Refused angles**

- "Whatnot is revolutionizing how America shops" — `slop`. Press-release framing; no journalist writes this headline.
- "Live shopping was declared dead in the US — Whatnot just proved everyone wrong" — `hallucinated_fact`. A contrarian angle needs a documented prevailing belief and evidence against it. No supplied fact establishes what "everyone" believed, and the $3B figure is self-reported, so the "proved" is doing unearned work.
- Separate "fashion angle" and "electronics angle" pitched as two stories — `duplicate`. Same lens, same protagonist (the category expansion), one story; splitting it by vertical is the spray-and-pray pattern.
- "Whatnot's $3B sets up an IPO" — `hallucinated_fact`. Nothing supplied says anything about an IPO, fundraising plans, or profitability.
- "What Whatnot's growth means for the future of retail" — `no_journalist_shape`. No sub-beat, no protagonist, no falsifiable claim — a topic, not a story.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Is the $3B gross merchandise volume or revenue, and over what period? Every kept angle leans on this number, and right now it's a company claim with no stated basis.
- What is the take-rate? "Commission on sales" with the rate undisclosed means the company's actual business size is unknown — and reporters on angles 1 and 4 will ask in the first email.
- What share of the $3B is still trading cards and collectibles? If fashion and electronics are a rounding error today, angle 2 is an aspiration, not a story.
- What does the median seller earn? If the honest answer is embarrassing, angle 3 will be written anyway — by someone else, without you.
- The "nearly $5B" valuation — from when? A stale mark changes how angle 4 reads.

**Next step:** `journalist-fit-check` — four viable shapes exist; pick the one whose proof you can actually produce, then resolve real names against it. Angles 1 and 2 are pitchable soonest; angles 3 and 4 require disclosures you haven't made yet.
