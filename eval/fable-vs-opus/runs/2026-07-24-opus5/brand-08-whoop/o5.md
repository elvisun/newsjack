**Whoop ships an FDA-cleared ECG and a blood-pressure feature that is deliberately not a medical device**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A digital-health regulatory reporter at a health-tech trade outlet or medtech newsletter who covers the FDA's device pathway and the line between "wellness" and "diagnostic." The story is the split inside one launch: one feature cleared by the FDA for detecting atrial fibrillation, the other launched as a non-medical wellness estimate requiring a cuff calibration. That is a live regulatory design decision, and this reporter can ask what determined which side of the line each feature landed on. Not for consumer gadget reviewers, general fitness press, or lifestyle desks — they will write a product roundup and miss the regulatory point.
- **Why now:** The launch is happening now, and both features ship together, which is what makes the contrast reportable rather than theoretical.
- **Decay:** 24hr — launch news. The regulatory framing can stretch a few days if the company will discuss the pathway on record; the announcement peg itself is a one-day event.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, regulator/rules protagonist. The story belongs to the FDA line, not to Whoop. The other angles here take the customer's side, the category's side, and the money's side.
- **Proof it needs:** the clearance documentation or clearance number and its exact indication wording; a plain statement of what "wellness estimate" means in labeling terms for the blood-pressure feature; someone at Whoop who will explain on record why blood pressure was not pursued as a cleared feature; the cardiologist advisory board's actual role in that decision.
- **Facts it rests on:** the ECG feature received FDA clearance for detecting atrial fibrillation; blood-pressure insights launch as a non-medical wellness estimate, not a diagnostic; the blood-pressure feature requires a one-time calibration with a cuff.

**A blood-pressure feature that needs a blood-pressure cuff to work**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-health or product-accountability reporter at a consumer-advocacy outlet or a health-desk that tests device claims, covering the gap between what wearable health features promise and what they actually deliver. The prevailing belief is that wearables are replacing clinical instruments; this feature requires the instrument it appears to replace and is explicitly not diagnostic. That is a reportable contradiction, not a complaint. Not for enthusiast wearable press or anyone doing an unboxing — they will treat the calibration step as a footnote.
- **Why now:** The feature is launching now, so the claim is fresh and testable before the reviews settle into consensus.
- **Decay:** week — contrarian and consumer-scrutiny pieces run past the launch cycle, and reviewers will need days with the device.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens. Every other angle treats the launch as a capability story; this one treats the calibration requirement and the non-diagnostic label as the story. Different protagonist entirely: the user who thinks they bought a blood-pressure monitor.
- **Proof it needs:** the exact consumer-facing wording of the wellness disclaimer and where it appears in the flow; what the calibration actually involves and how often it must be repeated; any accuracy data Whoop can share, with its methodology; whether the cardiologist advisory board reviewed the consumer framing or only the science. Be ready for a reporter to ask what happens when the estimate is wrong.
- **Facts it rests on:** blood-pressure insights launch as a non-medical wellness estimate, not a diagnostic; the feature requires a one-time calibration with a cuff; a cardiologist advisory board reviewed the features.
