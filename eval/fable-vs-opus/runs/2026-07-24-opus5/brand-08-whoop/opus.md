**Whoop ships FDA-cleared ECG — after the competition already did**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** A wearables/consumer-health-hardware reporter at a consumer-tech trade outlet or product desk that covers the Apple Watch / Samsung / Fitbit feature race. The honest hook is not "Whoop got FDA clearance" — it's that Whoop is arriving at AFib-detecting ECG *after* competitors who already ship it, so the real question is what Whoop is doing differently (form factor, screenless band, subscription model) to matter as a late entrant. Not for general business desks, not for medical-device trade press that wants a true diagnostic story, and not for AI/gadget-roundup writers who'll just list the spec.
- **Why now:** The feature is launching now and the FDA clearance is the peg; the "late to a crowded feature" framing is live this week.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard launch news; the catch-up framing fades fast once the launch cycle passes.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, competitor/incumbent protagonist — this is about the category's power balance and Whoop's position in it, not about Whoop's own milestone. It deliberately refuses the "first/breakthrough" frame the press release would want.
- **Proof it needs:** the specific competitors that already ship FDA-cleared ECG (Whoop's own positioning vs. named rivals); what Whoop's screenless-band ECG does differently from a wrist-screen device; the exact FDA clearance pathway/indication language; any device-design detail that explains why a late entrant is worth covering.
- **Facts it rests on:** the ECG feature received FDA clearance for detecting atrial fibrillation; competitors already ship FDA-cleared ECG; the features sit on Whoop's newest band.

**Whoop's new blood-pressure number isn't a medical reading — and needs a cuff to even start**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A health-tech accountability or consumer-health reporter at an outlet that covers the gap between wellness-wearable marketing and clinical reality (the same desk that scrutinizes sleep scores, "stress" metrics, and recovery numbers). The prevailing belief among buyers is that a wearable blood-pressure feature means real, usable blood-pressure monitoring. The fact cuts against it: Whoop's is an explicitly non-medical "wellness" estimate, not a diagnostic, and it can't function without a one-time cuff calibration — meaning you still need the medical device the wearable was supposed to replace. That tension is the story. Not for gadget reviewers who'll treat it as a checkbox feature, and not for pure medical-device press expecting a cleared diagnostic.
- **Why now:** The blood-pressure insights launch now; the "wellness estimate vs. real reading" question is most pointed at the moment of launch claims.
- **Decay:** week — the launch is the peg, but the broader "what does a non-diagnostic health number actually do for you" angle holds for a context piece.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion/contrarian lens — it names a real, widely-held assumption (a BP feature = BP monitoring) and uses Whoop's own framing as the evidence against it. Different protagonist and altitude from the ECG competition angle: here the subject is the claim itself, not Whoop's market position.
- **Proof it needs:** Whoop's exact on-record wording of the "wellness" / non-diagnostic disclaimer; how the cuff calibration works and how often it must be repeated; what the estimate is actually derived from; an independent clinician or hypertension expert willing to assess what a non-medical BP estimate is good for. (Contrarian angle — at least one of these proof items must be in hand before pitching.)
- **Facts it rests on:** blood-pressure insights are launching as a non-medical "wellness" estimate, not a diagnostic; they require a one-time calibration with a cuff.

**The newest heart features are locked behind Whoop's most expensive tier**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-subscription or "cost of the connected-health economy" reporter at a consumer-affairs or business-of-wellness outlet covering how health features are increasingly paywalled. The angle is the pricing structure: FDA-cleared heart-rhythm detection and a blood-pressure estimate are gated to the top membership tier, which raises a real consumer question about who can access health-adjacent features and at what price. Not for hardware-spec reviewers, not for clinical journals.
- **Why now:** The gating is part of this launch; the access-and-price story is timely against the launch but is the slower-burning of the set.
- **Decay:** week — tied to the launch peg, but the paywalled-health-features trend supports a standalone context piece for several days.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, customer/buyer protagonist (plus a relevance news-value tick) — the story is the person paying, not the device or the claim. Distinct from the ECG competition angle (which is about rivals) and the BP angle (which is about a claim's validity).
- **Proof it needs:** the actual price of the top tier and what else it includes; whether these features are available any other way; how the tier pricing compares to competitors' one-time-purchase or cheaper-tier health features; ideally a member or analyst who can speak to the cost calculus. The trend framing needs more than this one company to hold up across a week.
- **Facts it rests on:** the features are gated behind the most expensive membership tier; the features are FDA-cleared ECG (AFib) and a wellness blood-pressure estimate.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Which specific competitors already ship FDA-cleared ECG, and how does Whoop's version differ? Without a concrete differentiator, the counterposition angle is just "we're also doing the thing others did," which most reporters won't cover.
- What does a non-diagnostic blood-pressure "estimate" actually do for a user who already had to use a cuff to calibrate it? If there's no honest answer, the contrarian angle is the only honest framing — and it's not flattering.
- Can a cardiologist from the advisory board, or an independent clinician, go on record about the blood-pressure estimate's usefulness and limits? The contrarian angle is far weaker without an expert voice.
- What is the top-tier price, and is there any cheaper path to these features? The paywall angle lives or dies on the actual number.
- Is there any real news peg in the world right now (a regulatory move, a competitor launch, a heart-health awareness moment) that these features speak to? None was supplied, so every angle here rests on the launch itself — there is no breaking signal to justify a faster decay tag.
