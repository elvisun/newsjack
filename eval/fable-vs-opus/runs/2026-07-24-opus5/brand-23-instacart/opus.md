**Instacart's ad business hit a $1B run rate while its core delivery growth fell to single digits**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A retail/commerce business reporter at a markets or e-commerce trade outlet who covers the "retail media network" shift — the move by retailers to sell ads against their shopper data rather than make money on the goods themselves. The hook is the divergence: the supposedly core business (grocery delivery) is decelerating to single digits while ads are now a billion-dollar line. This reporter can test whether Instacart is quietly becoming an ad-tech company that happens to deliver groceries. Not for consumer "how to save on groceries" desks, not for general startup press.
- **Why now:** The $1B ad run-rate milestone is the disclosed peg, and the single-digit delivery growth is the tension that makes it a story rather than a press release. The contrast is live now while both numbers are current.
- **Decay:** 24hr — built on a disclosed company milestone; the contrarian framing keeps it alive slightly longer, but the news hook is the milestone disclosure.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion/contrarian lens. The prevailing belief is that Instacart is a grocery-delivery company; the fact that ads are at $1B while delivery growth has stalled to single digits is the evidence cutting against it. The other angles take ads or carts at face value — this one makes the *shift between the two* the story.
- **Proof it needs:** the actual ad revenue figure or run-rate definition behind "$1B" (run rate vs. trailing revenue); the specific "single digits" delivery growth figure and the period it covers; ideally the share of total revenue or profit ads now represent. A contrarian angle dies without the numbers that prove the divergence is real and not seasonal.
- **Facts it rests on:** advertising business hit a $1B annual run rate; grocery-delivery order growth has slowed to single digits; the company is publicly traded and profitable.

**Over 6,000 brands now advertise on a grocery-delivery app — what that says about retail media**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** An ad-tech / retail-media reporter at a marketing or advertising trade outlet covering where brand ad budgets are migrating. The entry point is the 6,000-brand number as evidence of how crowded and mainstream retail media has become — Instacart as one data point in a category-wide budget shift, not as the protagonist. This reporter cares about the market, not the company. Not for the markets desk that wants the stock story, and not for grocery-trade reporters focused on store operations.
- **Why now:** The 6,000-brand figure is freshly disclosed; the broader retail-media trend has a longer runway, so this can run as a context piece beyond the announcement day.
- **Decay:** week — a trend/context piece using the brand count as evidence rather than as breaking news.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder-of-abstraction lens, zoomed up. This makes the *retail-media category* the subject and uses Instacart's brand count as the floor under it. The contrarian angle is about Instacart's own internal divergence; this one is about the whole market.
- **Proof it needs:** how "6,000 brands" is counted (active advertisers in a period vs. ever-onboarded); comparable figures from other retail-media networks so the number means something in context; any spend or growth figure, since a bare advertiser count is scale without contextualization.
- **Facts it rests on:** ads now come from over 6,000 consumer brands; the advertising business hit a $1B annual run rate.

**Caper smart carts let shoppers skip the checkout line — and they're spreading to more chains**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A grocery / retail-operations trade reporter covering in-store technology and the long-running "frictionless checkout" race (the category Amazon's cashierless stores helped define). The hook is the Caper cart's expansion to more banners and what skip-the-line checkout does to store labor and the front-end. Not for the ad-tech desk, not for the markets desk — this is an operations and store-floor story.
- **Why now:** The carts are being rolled out to more grocery chains now; the expansion is the timely event.
- **Decay:** 24hr — a rollout/expansion announcement decays on the normal product-news cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the retailer/store as protagonist, and the relevance news-value for store operators. It's about hardware on the store floor, a different beat and a different protagonist than the ad-business and market-shift angles.
- **Proof it needs:** how many stores and which named chains are getting carts in this wave (vs. "a few hundred" already live); what the carts cost a retailer; any data on checkout-time reduction, labor impact, or basket-size lift; a named retailer willing to describe results. Without a result, this is a product description, not a story.
- **Facts it rests on:** the Caper smart carts track items as shoppers add them and skip the checkout line; smart carts are live in a few hundred stores with plans to expand; the company partners with more than 1,500 retail banners.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- What is the actual ad revenue number and how is "$1B run rate" defined? A run rate annualized from one strong quarter is a weaker claim than trailing revenue, and the contrarian angle lives or dies on it.
- What exactly are the "single digit" growth figures for delivery, and over what period? If it's a known seasonal dip rather than a structural slowdown, the strongest (contrarian) angle collapses.
- Is any retailer running Caper carts willing to be named and to share a result — checkout-time cut, labor change, basket lift? Without one, the cart angle is a spec sheet.
- How is "6,000 brands" counted, and how does it compare to Amazon's or Walmart's retail-media networks? The number is meaningless to a reporter without a benchmark.
- These are disclosed company numbers with no current external news peg supplied. Is there a live earnings event, a competitor move, or a market story this should be tied to? If not, every angle here is standard company news, not a newsjack.
