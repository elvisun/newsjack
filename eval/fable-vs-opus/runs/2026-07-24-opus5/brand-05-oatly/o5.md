**Oatly cuts the carbon footprint of its barista oat milk by 31% while closing a US plant**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A food-and-beverage business reporter at a CPG trade outlet or a business desk covering plant-based category economics — the kind of reporter who has been writing "the plant-based boom is over" stories and tracking Oatly's losses. The prevailing belief is that unprofitable plant-based companies retreat from sustainability spending when they cut costs; here the same quarter delivers a verified footprint reduction and a plant closure. That tension is the story, not the product. Not for consumer food bloggers, product-review desks, or coffee lifestyle press.
- **Why now:** The reformulation, the chain rollout, and the plant closure are all live at the same time. The contrast only exists while both are current.
- **Decay:** 24hr — a company-news window; the reporter needs the closure and the reformulation to still be same-cycle news to write the tension.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens. Every other angle here treats the footprint number or the partnership as the subject; this one treats the *combination* of cost-cutting and sustainability investment as the subject, and Oatly's own financials as the evidence.
- **Proof it needs:** the closure timeline, which plant, headcount affected, and whether production shifts to the remaining two; whether the reformulation is manufactured at the closing plant; the actual operating loss figures for the last two quarters, not "narrowing"; an executive who will answer the cost question on record rather than deflecting to the LCA.
- **Facts it rests on:** 31% lower climate footprint per liter than the previous formula, verified by a named third-party LCA firm; Oatly remains unprofitable with a narrowing operating loss last quarter; one of three US plants is closing to cut costs.

**What a 31% footprint cut actually measures — and what an LCA leaves out**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A sustainability or climate-accounting reporter at a food-industry trade publication or a corporate-climate newsletter, the kind who scrutinizes Scope 3 claims and third-party verification. The hook is methodological: a per-liter reduction against a company's own prior formula is a specific, checkable claim, and this reporter's readers argue about exactly what counts inside that boundary. Not for general business desks or consumer press, which will flatten it into "greener oat milk."
- **Why now:** The claim is being made publicly now, which is when a verification-minded reporter can still put questions to the LCA firm before it becomes settled marketing copy.
- **Decay:** week — methodology pieces run on a slower clock than the launch, but the news peg fades.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, change-over-time cut, with the number itself as the subject rather than the product or the buyer. Distinct from the contrarian angle, which uses the number as one half of a contrast, not as the thing being examined.
- **Proof it needs:** the LCA report itself, not a summary — boundaries, functional unit, baseline year, allocation method; the LCA firm's name and whether it will speak independently; whether the study is third-party *verified* or third-party *conducted*; what drove the 31% (oat sourcing, energy, formulation, transport); the absolute figure per liter, since a percentage without a baseline is unreviewable; whether the closing plant's footprint is inside or outside the boundary.
- **Facts it rests on:** 31% lower climate footprint per liter than the previous formula; verified by a named third-party LCA firm; the plant closure and unprofitability are relevant only as boundary questions.
