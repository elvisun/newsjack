**Duolingo says people are talking to its cartoon owl for nine minutes at a time**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** A consumer-tech or app-economy reporter at a business outlet covering engagement mechanics in subscription apps. The number is the story: 9 minutes per session in beta is a long time for a voice interaction in a mobile app, and this reporter can test whether that number means learning or means the app found a new way to hold attention. Not for education-policy desks, not for AI-infrastructure reporters, not for K-12 trade press.
- **Why now:** The feature is moving from beta to a paid launch, so the beta number is being published for the first time.
- **Decay:** 24hr — a launch-day metric. The number gets old fast unless a reporter can compare it against something.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, scale rung — this angle is about the session-length figure itself, not the product or the company's growth. The other angles use different protagonists and don't rest on this number.
- **Proof it needs:** the beta sample size and length of the beta window; what counts as a "session" and whether it includes idle time; comparable session lengths for Duolingo's existing lesson formats so 9 minutes has context; whether the beta group was self-selected. Without the baseline, this is a number, not a story.
- **Facts it rests on:** engagement on the new video feature in beta averaged 9 minutes per session; paid Max subscribers can have live spoken video conversations with the character Lily.

**Duolingo puts its most-criticized design behind a live voice interface**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education-technology reporter at an ed-tech trade outlet or the education desk of a general-interest paper, covering the efficacy debate around gamified learning. The prevailing belief this cuts against is the educators' critique that gamification produces shallow learning; a live, unscripted spoken conversation is the one format that gamified drilling is usually accused of not producing. This reporter can put the company's claim and the critics' claim in the same piece. Not for consumer app reviewers, not for AI-product press, and not for anyone who wants a friendly product story — this one has a real opposing side and Duolingo will be asked to defend it.
- **Why now:** The video-call feature is launching now, which forces the efficacy question into the open rather than leaving it as a standing complaint.
- **Decay:** week — the efficacy debate isn't tied to launch day, but the launch is what gives a reporter a reason to open the file this week.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens — this is the only angle where the critics are part of the story rather than a risk to be managed. It has an adversary; the data and business angles don't.
- **Proof it needs:** who the critical educators are and what they actually said, with sources — you cannot pitch a rebuttal to criticism you can't quote; any internal or third-party evidence that conversation practice improves speaking outcomes; a spokesperson prepared to be quoted answering the criticism directly rather than deflecting to engagement numbers; whether the company has ever commissioned or published efficacy research. If the answer to the last two is no, this angle turns into a story about a company that dodged the question. Do not pitch it until you know.
- **Facts it rests on:** paid Max subscribers can have live spoken video conversations for conversation practice; some educators have publicly criticized gamification as shallow learning; engagement in beta averaged 9 minutes per session.

**A profitable public company is giving away chess and charging for the AI**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A markets or equity-research reporter covering consumer-subscription businesses, or a business-strategy desk that follows public consumer-software names. The hook is the split: the free product expands the top of the funnel into a category that isn't language at all, while the expensive AI feature sits behind the Max tier. For a reporter covering a profitable public company, that is a readable monetization thesis with numbers attached to it. Not for education reporters, not for product reviewers, not for chess press.
- **Why now:** Both moves are being announced together, which is what makes the free-versus-paid contrast legible instead of speculative.
- **Decay:** week — the announcement is a 24-hour event, but the monetization question stays open until the next earnings report.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, investor/market protagonist — the reader here is someone deciding what the business is worth, not someone deciding whether to learn Spanish. The data angle is about user behavior; this one is about the P&L.
- **Proof it needs:** Max pricing and what share of DAU is on Max; the cost side of running live video inference against subscription revenue; whether the 40% DAU growth is disclosed in a filing or is a company statement, and the absolute number behind "a new high"; whether chess is expected to convert to paid or is purely a retention play. A reporter on this beat will check the filing before they check the pitch, so the numbers must match the filing exactly.
- **Facts it rests on:** chess course launches free for all users; the video calls are limited to paid Max subscribers; daily active users grew 40% year over year to a new high; the company is publicly traded and profitable.

**The language app that decided it isn't a language app**

- **Story type:** category-creation
- **Why a journalist cares:** A reporter covering the consumer-app market or the learning-software category for a business or strategy outlet — someone who writes about what a company is becoming, not what it shipped. Chess is not a language, and adding it says the company thinks its asset is the habit loop, not the subject matter. This reporter can ask what else fits in that container and who the competitors become. Not for language-learning trade press, not for the chess world, not for AI reporters.
- **Why now:** No urgent hook. The chess launch is the occasion, but the argument would hold a month from now. Pitch this as a thinking piece, not a news item.
- **Decay:** month — category arguments run when a desk has room, not on launch day.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed up — the subject is the category boundary, not the product, the number, or the balance sheet. It's the only angle where Duolingo's language business is the thing being questioned.
- **Proof it needs:** an executive on record on why chess and what the selection criteria for a non-language course are; whether more non-language courses are planned; any evidence about what the existing music or math courses did for retention, if such courses exist — do not assert they do without checking; a defensible statement of what the company now considers its market. Without an on-record executive this is an outsider's think piece, not your pitch.
- **Facts it rests on:** a chess course launches free for all users; daily active users grew 40% year over year to a new high.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Is the 40% DAU figure from a filing or from a company statement? Every business-desk reporter will check, and a mismatch between your pitch and the 10-Q is the fastest way to lose that desk permanently.
- What is the baseline session length for a normal Duolingo lesson? If it's close to 9 minutes, the beta number proves nothing and the data angle collapses.
- Do you have any efficacy evidence at all, or only engagement numbers? The educators' critique is precisely that engagement is not learning. Answering an efficacy question with a session-length metric confirms the critique rather than rebutting it.
- Which educators criticized gamification, when, and where? You cannot build the contrarian angle on a critique you can only paraphrase.
- Will a spokesperson actually take the shallow-learning question on the record, or will they bridge to growth numbers? If it's the latter, drop the contrarian angle — a reporter who senses a dodge writes the dodge.
- Was the beta group self-selected enthusiasts? If yes, say so before a reporter finds out, not after.
