**Databricks discloses a $3B run rate while signaling an IPO it won't put a date on**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A markets or IPO-watch reporter at a financial outlet covering late-stage private companies and the IPO pipeline. The hook is the disclosure itself: a privately held company last valued at $62B volunteering revenue ($3B annualized) and growth (over 60% year over year) while signaling an eventual IPO with no date. That combination — selective financial disclosure plus IPO signaling — is exactly what this beat scrutinizes: what's being shown, what's being withheld, and why now. Not for AI product reporters, developer-tools press, or enterprise-IT trade desks.
- **Why now:** The revenue figure and the IPO signal were disclosed alongside today's model release; the disclosure is the news event.
- **Decay:** 24hr — self-reported financial milestones from a private company get covered on the day or folded into the next IPO-pipeline roundup.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, investor/money protagonist. The model release is background here; the story is the financial disclosure and what it telegraphs about IPO readiness. The other kept angles treat the model as the story.
- **Proof it needs:** clarity on what "annualized revenue" means (which period, annualized how); whether the figure is audited or company-stated; an on-record executive willing to discuss IPO timing posture, even to say "no date."
- **Facts it rests on:** crossed $3B in annualized revenue; revenue grew over 60% year over year; privately held, last valued at $62B; the company has signaled an eventual IPO without a date.

**Databricks open-sources a foundation model and aims it at closed-API vendors' regulated customers**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** An enterprise-AI reporter at a business-tech outlet who covers the competitive balance between open and closed model providers. The conflict is explicit and supplied: Databricks positions the model as an alternative to closed APIs for regulated enterprises. That's a direct shot at the closed-API business model in the customer segment where data-control objections bite hardest. The reporter can test whether closed-API vendors are actually losing regulated accounts. Not for consumer-AI press, gadget desks, or general startup blogs.
- **Why now:** The release is live now; counterposition angles run with the announcement or not at all.
- **Decay:** 24hr — competitive-positioning stories ride the announcement cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, conflict — the protagonist is the open-vs-closed power balance, not Databricks' financials (angle 1) or the buyer's compliance pain (angle 3).
- **Proof it needs:** the exact license terms (a reporter will check whether "open license" survives scrutiny against open-source definitions); at least one regulated enterprise that switched from a closed API or evaluated both; independent benchmark replication — the supplied numbers are the company's own and cannot carry the pitch.
- **Facts it rests on:** the model is released under an open license for enterprises to fine-tune on their own data; the company positions it as an alternative to closed APIs for regulated enterprises; benchmark numbers are the company's own.

**For banks and hospitals, the AI question is custody, not capability**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A reporter at a regulated-industry trade outlet — banking technology, healthcare IT, insurance tech — covering how compliance teams adopt AI. The protagonist is the regulated buyer: an enterprise that cannot send sensitive data to a third-party API and needs to fine-tune on its own data inside its own perimeter. The release is one data point in a story this beat is already telling about data custody and model control. Not for VC press, markets reporters, or general AI-news desks.
- **Why now:** The release gives the trend a fresh, concrete artifact, but the underlying custody story has a longer runway than the announcement.
- **Decay:** week — trend pieces about regulated-enterprise AI adoption can run after the announcement day if a real buyer is on record.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed down to the buyer's altitude, combined with the relevance news value — a materially affected audience slice. Angle 2 is about who loses among vendors; this is about what the buyer actually gets.
- **Proof it needs:** a named regulated enterprise willing to describe its data-custody constraint and its fine-tuning plan — without one, this angle is a topic, not a story; specifics on what the open license permits for fine-tuned derivatives.
- **Facts it rests on:** the model is released under an open license for enterprises to fine-tune on their own data; the company positions it as an alternative to closed APIs for regulated enterprises.

**Databricks gives its model away while revenue grows 60% — a test of where the money in AI actually sits**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** A business-of-AI reporter at an analytical business outlet or paid tech newsletter who covers AI economics and moats. The prevailing belief: proprietary frontier models are the core asset, and giving one away destroys value. The supplied evidence cutting against it: a company open-sourcing a foundation model in the same breath as disclosing $3B in annualized revenue growing over 60%. The honest version of this story interrogates whether the model is the product or the marketing — and Databricks has handed over the raw material for that question. Not for trade compliance desks or breaking-news AI reporters.
- **Why now:** Both halves of the contrast — the giveaway and the revenue number — landed in the same announcement; the juxtaposition is the hook.
- **Decay:** week — analysis pieces about open-model economics outlive the announcement day.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens. Angle 1 reads the revenue number as IPO signaling; this reads the same number against the giveaway and asks what business model reconciles them. Different question, different beat.
- **Proof it needs:** any breakdown of what drives the $3B — the angle collapses if the company won't say how an open model makes money; an on-record executive articulating the economics; the company's own framing of why open release helps rather than hurts revenue.
- **Facts it rests on:** the model is released under an open license; crossed $3B in annualized revenue; revenue grew over 60% year over year.

**Refused angles**

- "Databricks model outperforms closed rivals on benchmarks" — `hallucinated_fact`. The benchmark numbers are the company's own with no independent verification supplied. A headline asserting superiority treats unverified marketing as fact; a reporter will reproduce or discard the numbers.
- "Databricks IPO is coming" — `hallucinated_fact`. The supplied fact is a signal with no date. Any angle implying timing invents the one thing the company explicitly withheld.
- "Databricks revolutionizes enterprise AI with a game-changing open model" — `slop`. Press-release framing; failed the rewrite because there is nothing under it that angle 2 doesn't already carry.
- "What this means for AI startups / the open-source movement" — `no_journalist_shape`. "AI reporter" and "the ecosystem" are not beats; the defensible versions of this story are angles 2 and 4.
- "The $62B decacorn's next chapter" — `duplicate` of angle 1 with a vaguer protagonist and no new fact.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Is the license actually open by any recognized definition, or open-weights with commercial restrictions? This is the first thing every reporter on angles 2 and 3 will check, and the positioning collapses if the answer is fuzzy.
- Will you publish benchmark methodology so the numbers can be independently reproduced? Until then they are unusable in any pitch.
- What does "annualized revenue" mean precisely — which period, annualized how — and is it audited? A markets reporter will ask before printing the $3B figure.
- Can you name one regulated enterprise that is actually fine-tuning this model on its own data? Without one, angle 3 dies and angle 2 weakens badly.
- Does the open model generate revenue, or is it marketing for something else? If you can't answer on record, angle 4 becomes a hostile story someone else writes.
