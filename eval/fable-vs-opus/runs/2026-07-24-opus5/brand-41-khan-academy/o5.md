**Khan Academy's AI tutor reaches 1 million more students before the research is in**

- **Story type:** contrarian
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education-technology reporter at a K-12 trade outlet or education policy publication who covers district procurement and evidence standards. The tension is the story: a nonprofit with unusual credibility is scaling an AI tutor to 30 districts while publishing results it produced itself and has not put through peer review. That reporter can ask what evidence bar districts are applying before they hand a chatbot to a classroom. Not for consumer AI press, general startup coverage, or product-review desks — they will write the launch, not the question.
- **Why now:** The 30-district rollout and the study are being published together right now, which is exactly when the evidence question is answerable and unanswered.
- **Decay:** 24hr — the rollout announcement is a one-day news event; a reporter who wants the evidence frame needs to reach the district and research sources on the same cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Inversion lens. The prevailing belief is that a nonprofit's free tool sidesteps the scrutiny applied to commercial edtech; the fact that the study is Khan's own and not peer-reviewed cuts against it. Every other angle below treats the rollout as a thing that happened; this one treats the evidence gap as the thing that happened.
- **Proof it needs:** the study itself, in full — methodology, sample size, control or comparison group, how "extra weeks of learning" was defined and measured; whether it has been submitted anywhere for peer review and to whom; what Khan tells districts about the evidence's current status; an external education researcher willing to read it on record. Do not pitch this angle without the methodology in hand — a reporter will ask in the first reply.
- **Facts it rests on:** an internal study across pilot districts found students who used it regularly gained the equivalent of extra weeks of learning, by Khan's own measure; the study is not yet peer-reviewed; the rollout reaches 30 more US school districts and an estimated 1 million additional students.

**What a district actually got: inside the first classrooms to use Khanmigo at scale**

- **Story type:** customer-story
- **Why a journalist cares:** A K-12 reporter at a local or regional outlet covering the specific district, or a classroom-practice reporter at an education trade publication. The concrete rung is the draw: which grades, which subjects, what teachers changed about their lesson planning, what happened when a student used the tutor instead of asking the teacher. A district superintendent or a named teacher makes this reportable; without one, it is a press release. Not for national business desks or AI-industry press.
- **Why now:** The rollout is live and the pilot districts have a full stretch of classroom use behind them — the first cohort can describe what actually happened rather than what was promised.
- **Decay:** week — classroom-practice features run on a slower clock than the announcement, and the pilot experience stays reportable after the news day passes.
- **What makes it distinct:** Ladder lens, zoomed all the way down — one district, one classroom, one teacher's changed workflow. The rollout angle is about scale; this one is about what a single Tuesday looks like.
- **Proof it needs:** at least one named district and one named teacher cleared to speak; specifics on grade levels and subjects; what the teacher-facing lesson-planning feature actually produces; usage data for that district (how many students, how often, over what period); a student or parent voice if the district permits it.
- **Facts it rests on:** Khanmigo gives students step-by-step tutoring and gives teachers lesson-planning help; the rollout reaches 30 more US school districts; an internal study across pilot districts covered students who used it regularly.

**Teachers are asking whether an AI tutor teaches students to think or to ask**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education reporter covering teaching practice and learning science, or a higher-ed/K-12 columnist at an outlet that runs argument pieces about pedagogy. The hook is the educator objection already attached to this rollout: whether step-by-step tutoring builds independent thinking or trains dependence. That is a live disagreement among practitioners, and a 1-million-student rollout gives the argument a specific object. Not for reporters looking for a product story, and not for anyone who wants Khan Academy to be the villain — the honest version has Khan answering the critique, not dodging it.
- **Why now:** The scale-up gives the pedagogical argument a concrete instance rather than a hypothetical one; the objection is being raised now, against this rollout.
- **Decay:** week — the debate outlasts the announcement, but its news value is highest while the rollout is the reference point.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the skeptical educator as protagonist, plus news-value conflict. The contrarian angle above is about evidence quality; this one is about the pedagogy itself — a tool could be well-evidenced on test gains and still be the wrong shape for independent thinking. Different objection, different expert, different beat.
- **Proof it needs:** an actual named educator making the independent-thinking critique on record — the update says "some educators question" and a reporter will want the some; Khan's own product answer, specifically how the step-by-step tutoring is designed to withhold answers rather than supply them; any measurement in the study of what students could do without the tutor afterward; a learning-science researcher who can situate the argument. Do not run this angle with an anonymous "critics say."
- **Facts it rests on:** some educators question AI tutoring's effect on independent thinking; Khanmigo gives students step-by-step tutoring; the rollout reaches an estimated 1 million additional students.

**A donor-funded nonprofit is giving away AI tutoring that competitors sell**

- **Story type:** counterposition
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education-business or edtech-market reporter covering district purchasing, vendor competition, and how school budgets get spent. The economic fact is the story: Khanmigo is free to teachers and paid for by donors, at a moment when the same capability is a paid product elsewhere. That reporter can ask what free-at-the-point-of-use does to a district's willingness to buy, and what happens to the districts if the donor money changes. Not for classroom-practice reporters or education-policy desks focused on curriculum — this is a market-structure story.
- **Why now:** The rollout is the moment the free offer moves from pilot scale to 30 districts and roughly a million students, which is the scale at which the funding model becomes a legitimate question rather than a curiosity.
- **Decay:** week — market-structure pieces can run past the announcement day if the funding detail is on record.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens with the money as protagonist, and news-value conflict. The evidence angle questions the study; this one questions the sustainability and market effect of giving the product away. No overlap in beat, source list, or what the reporter has to verify.
- **Proof it needs:** who the donors are and how long the funding is committed; annual cost to run Khanmigo per student or per district; what districts pay, if anything, for the parts that are not free to teachers; whether Khan will state on record what happens to these districts if donor funding ends. Any claim about what competitors charge must come from a source you can hand over — do not assert competitor pricing you have not verified.
- **Facts it rests on:** Khan Academy is a nonprofit; Khanmigo is offered to teachers for free, funded by donors; the rollout reaches 30 more US school districts and an estimated 1 million additional students.

**Refused angles**

- "Khan Academy's AI tutor delivers weeks of extra learning" — `hallucinated_fact`. The update says extra weeks of learning "by Khan's own measure," from an unreviewed internal study. Stating the result as a finding, in a headline, launders a self-produced number into an established one. The number can appear in a pitch only as "our internal study, methodology attached, not yet peer-reviewed."
- "Khanmigo is transforming how a million students learn" — `slop`. Press-release framing, no verb a reporter would use, and it asserts an outcome the evidence does not support.
- "Khan Academy proves AI tutoring works at scale" — `hallucinated_fact`. One internal, non-peer-reviewed study of pilot districts does not support a category-wide claim, and a reporter will treat the overreach as a reason to distrust everything else in the pitch.
- "The nonprofit model is the future of AI in education" — `no_journalist_shape`. No protagonist, no beat, no verifiable claim. This is a thesis a columnist might arrive at, not a story you can hand them.
- "How Khanmigo helps teachers save time on lesson planning" — `duplicate`. Same lens and same protagonist as the classroom angle above, at a weaker altitude. The classroom angle absorbs it; keep the teacher's lesson-planning workflow as a section of that story, not a separate pitch.
- "AI tutoring arrives as schools debate AI in the classroom" — `no_why_now_but_required`. There is no supplied current news signal here. A general sense that AI in schools is much-discussed is not a peg, and a reporter reads a manufactured one immediately. If a real signal exists — a state guidance release, a district policy vote, a research publication this week — it would sharpen the first and third angles substantially, but it has to be real.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Will you release the study's full methodology to a reporter who asks? If the answer is no, or not yet, do not pitch the results — a reporter who requests methodology and gets a summary will write about the refusal instead of the finding. The first angle is only strong if you can hand over the document.
- How is "the equivalent of extra weeks of learning" calculated? Against what baseline, over what period, with what comparison group, and how were students who "used it regularly" selected? Self-selection alone could produce this result — a reporter with any research literacy will ask within one exchange.
- Has any of this been submitted for peer review, and to whom? "Not yet peer-reviewed" reads very differently from "under review at a named journal."
- Do you have a named district and a named teacher cleared by their communications office to speak on record? Without one, two of the four angles above are unpitchable.
- Can you name a specific educator making the independent-thinking critique, and are you willing to have a reporter interview them? If you would rather the critique not appear, understand that a reporter will find it anyway, and the story is much better for you if you brought it up first.
- Is the 1 million students an enrollment number or an actual-usage projection? Reach figures that turn out to be seat counts are a standard reporter trap, and the correction lands after publication.
- Who funds this, and for how long? A district deciding to build Khanmigo into instruction is making a multi-year commitment against a grant you may not control past next year.
- I have no current news signal for this rollout and no prior-coverage links to check, so nothing here is anchored to a breaking story. Every angle above rests on your own announcement plus the classroom results.
