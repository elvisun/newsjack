**AI tutoring is about to reach a million more public-school students — through 30 districts at once**

- **Story type:** trend
- **Why a journalist cares:** A K-12 edtech reporter at an education trade outlet covering district technology adoption. The hook is not the product — it's the adoption pattern: 30 districts signing on simultaneously is a measurable data point on how fast AI tutoring is moving into public schools. Not for consumer-tech desks, general AI press, or business reporters who only cover funding rounds.
- **Why now:** The 30-district rollout is being announced now; district-adoption stories run on the announcement.
- **Decay:** 24hr — standard company-news cycle for a rollout announcement; the adoption-trend framing buys a little slack but not a week.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, magnitude — contextualized scale (1 million additional students, 30 districts) is the story, not the product or the study. The other kept angles center the evidence, the money, and the skeptics.
- **Proof it needs:** the list of the 30 districts, or at least several named ones; the basis for the 1-million-student estimate; whether districts are signing up proactively or being recruited.
- **Facts it rests on:** rollout to 30 more US school districts; estimated 1 million additional students reached; Khanmigo gives students step-by-step tutoring and teachers lesson-planning help.

**Khan Academy says its AI tutor added weeks of learning — by its own yardstick**

- **Story type:** data
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education-research or edtech-efficacy reporter at an outlet that covers evidence standards in education — the kind who reads methodology sections. The story is the first classroom results *and* their limits in the same breath: an internal study, not peer-reviewed, measuring gains "by Khan's own measure." That tension is exactly this beat's bread and butter. Not for general tech press that will print the headline number uncritically, and not for product-review outlets.
- **Why now:** The results are being published now alongside the rollout; first-results moments only happen once.
- **Decay:** week — data and evidence pieces can run for days after the announcement, especially if the methodology is made available.
- **What makes it distinct:** Data lens, change-over-time — the learning-gains number is the protagonist. Distinct from the scale angle (adoption, not efficacy) and the skeptic angle (pedagogy, not measurement). This angle only works if Khan leads with the caveats rather than burying them.
- **Proof it needs:** the full study — sample size, comparison group, what "regular use" means, what unit "weeks of learning" converts from; whether the study will be submitted for peer review; a researcher (internal or external) willing to discuss the methodology on record.
- **Facts it rests on:** internal study across pilot districts found regular users gained the equivalent of extra weeks of learning, by Khan's own measure; the study is not yet peer-reviewed; Khan Academy is publishing the results.

**Donors, not school districts, are footing the bill for this AI tutor**

- **Story type:** funding-mechanics
- **Why a journalist cares:** A philanthropy reporter, or an edtech-business reporter covering who pays for AI in classrooms. The money is the hook: a nonprofit giving an AI tutor to teachers for free, funded by donors, reaching a million more students. That's a different economic model from a procurement sale, and this beat can test whether donor funding can sustain AI at that scale. Not for classroom-practice outlets or general education desks that don't cover the business side.
- **Why now:** The rollout makes the funding model's load-bearing moment concrete — a million more students is a real cost question, not a hypothetical.
- **Decay:** week — the funding-model story keys off the rollout but doesn't expire with the news cycle.
- **What makes it distinct:** Perspective lens, the money as protagonist — neither the students, the study, nor the skeptics. No other kept angle touches the nonprofit/donor structure.
- **Proof it needs:** who the donors are and whether any will be named; what the rollout costs (compute, support, training); whether free-to-teachers holds at 1M-student scale or has a horizon.
- **Facts it rests on:** Khan Academy is a nonprofit; Khanmigo is offered to teachers for free, funded by donors; the rollout reaches an estimated 1 million additional students.

**As Khanmigo scales, Khan Academy answers the independent-thinking critics directly**

- **Story type:** defensive-comment
- **Why a journalist cares:** An education-policy or teaching-practice reporter covering the AI-in-classrooms debate. The skepticism — does AI tutoring undermine independent thinking — is already a live argument among educators, and this rollout gives it a concrete, million-student test case. The angle is Khan engaging the criticism on record, not dodging it: what step-by-step tutoring is designed to do and not do. Not for edtech cheerleader outlets, and not for reporters who only want the announcement.
- **Why now:** The rollout raises the stakes of an existing educator concern from pilot-scale to a million students; the debate has a fresh, concrete subject.
- **Decay:** week — debate pieces follow the announcement but have a longer tail than the rollout news itself.
- **What makes it distinct:** News-values lens, conflict — the educators' objection is the engine, and Khan is the respondent. The data angle is about measurement quality; this one is about pedagogy, with a different protagonist (the skeptical educators) and a different desk.
- **Proof it needs:** a Khan spokesperson willing to address the independent-thinking concern specifically and on record; any design detail showing how the tutor pushes students to work rather than handing answers; ideally a skeptical educator willing to be quoted, so the piece isn't a one-sided rebuttal.
- **Facts it rests on:** some educators question AI tutoring's effect on independent thinking; Khanmigo gives students step-by-step tutoring; the rollout reaches an estimated 1 million additional students.

**Refused angles**

- "Khanmigo is transforming how America learns" — `slop`.
- "Study proves AI tutoring works" — `hallucinated_fact`. The study is internal, not peer-reviewed, and measured by Khan's own yardstick. "Proves" is a claim the facts can't carry, and a reporter will say so in print.
- "Inside one pilot district's Khanmigo classrooms" — `hallucinated_fact`. No district, teacher, or student is named in the supplied facts. Bring a named district willing to talk and this becomes the strongest angle in the set.
- "One million more students get an AI tutor" as a standalone magnitude piece — `duplicate` of the adoption-trend angle; same lens, same protagonist, weaker shape.
- "Khanmigo and the back-to-school AI wave" — `no_why_now_but_required`. The facts don't say when the rollout lands relative to the school year, and no current back-to-school signal was supplied. Don't manufacture a calendar peg.

**Uncomfortable questions**

- Will you release the study's methodology — sample size, comparison group, definition of "regular use," and what "extra weeks of learning" converts from? If a reporter asks and the answer is no, the data angle flips from asset to liability.
- Is the study going to peer review? Publishing headline results before peer review invites the evidence-standards story to run *about* you instead of *with* you.
- Can any of the 30 districts — or any pilot district from the study — be named and put on record? Every angle here gets materially stronger with one.
- Is your spokesperson actually prepared to engage the independent-thinking criticism, or will they retreat to talking points? The defensive-comment angle is worse than nothing if the answers are evasive.
- Who are the donors funding the free rollout, and will any be named? The funding-mechanics angle is thin without them.
